## 1. What randomised or controlled studies exist on hydration apps, reminders, smart bottles and workplace water interventions?

### Takeaway
The trial evidence is thin and mostly in kidney-stone patients: smart bottles and reminder programmes raise intake or urine volume by roughly 0.3-0.6 L/day more than advice alone in the short term (6-12 weeks), but the one large long-term trial (PUSH, n=1,658, Lancet 2026) found only a modest urine-volume gain and no reduction in stone recurrence. In healthy adults I found only one tiny uncontrolled-comparison pilot (n=22 women, 5 weeks), no adequately powered RCT of a consumer hydration app, and no trial with objective long-term adherence data.

### Cited Findings

**Smart bottle RCT in stone formers (Stout et al. 2022) [patients, small, sponsor-linked]**
- 85 adults with nephrolithiasis and low urine volume (<1.5 L) randomised to dietary recommendations only (n=44) or recommendations plus a smart water bottle (n=41); 24-hour urine collected at 6 and 12 weeks. — [Univ. of Minnesota Experts record of Stout et al., J Ren Nutr 2022;32(4):389-395](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/a-randomized-trial-evaluating-the-use-of-a-smart-water-bottle-to-/)
- Mean change in 24-h urine volume: +1.37 L in the smart-bottle arm (95% CI -0.51 to 3.25) vs +0.79 L with advice only (95% CI -1.15 to 2.73), P = .04. Note both confidence intervals include zero, so the between-arm difference of about 0.58 L is imprecise. — [UMN Experts record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/a-randomized-trial-evaluating-the-use-of-a-smart-water-bottle-to-/)
- Follow-up urine collections were available for only 51 of 85 patients (about 40% missing); bottle usage/adherence is not reported in the abstract. — [UMN Experts record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/a-randomized-trial-evaluating-the-use-of-a-smart-water-bottle-to-/)
- "Forgetting to drink" was the main limiting factor for 60% of all participants at baseline; in the smart-bottle arm it fell from 68.4% to 45.4% (P < .05), in the advice arm from 51.2% to 40.0% (P = .13, not significant). — [UMN Experts record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/a-randomized-trial-evaluating-the-use-of-a-smart-water-bottle-to-/)
- The abstract states the bottle's manufacturer sponsored the study but did not financially support it. The manufacturer (HidrateSpark) publicises the result as "1.3 liters vs. 0.8 liters" on its own blog — treat that page as marketing. — [UMN Experts record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/a-randomized-trial-evaluating-the-use-of-a-smart-water-bottle-to-/); [HidrateSpark blog](https://hidratespark.com/blogs/hidrate-spark/use-of-hidratespark-2-0-improves-fluid-intake-levels-for-study-participants-who-form-kidney-stones)

**PUSH trial (Desai et al., The Lancet, online 19 March 2026) [patients, age 12+, large, NIH-funded network]**
- 1,658 adolescents and adults (age 12+) with a stone history and low urine volume, six US academic centres, enrolled 2017-2024. Intervention: Bluetooth smart bottle + personalised "fluid prescription" + financial incentives tied to it + adaptive health coaching + text reminders. Control: a smart bottle without prescription plus guideline care. — [CHOP news release](https://www.chop.edu/news/behavioral-hydration-program-landmark-kidney-stone-prevention-trial-increased-urine-output)
- Result: the programme "modestly increased urine output" but did not reduce symptomatic stone recurrence; the release gives no numbers. Citation: "Prevention of Urinary Stones with Hydration: A Randomised Clinical Trial of an Adherence Intervention", DOI 10.1016/S0140-6736(25)02637-6. — [CHOP news release](https://www.chop.edu/news/behavioral-hydration-program-landmark-kidney-stone-prevention-trial-increased-urine-output)
- Recurrence over two years was similar between groups and imaging outcomes (new stones, stone growth) did not differ **[secondary: search summary of trade press]**. — [Urology Times, 20 March 2026](https://www.urologytimes.com/view/behavioral-hydration-program-falls-short-in-reducing-kidney-stone-recurrence); [Cleveland Clinic Consult QD](https://consultqd.clevelandclinic.org/study-raises-new-questions-about-achieving-adequate-fluid-intake-for-kidney-stones)
- Lead investigator's framing: the team needs to "understand why adherence breaks down"; the release says the findings do not overturn guideline advice to increase fluids. — [CHOP news release](https://www.chop.edu/news/behavioral-hydration-program-landmark-kidney-stone-prevention-trial-increased-urine-output)

**sipIT (Conroy et al.) [patients; efficacy results not yet found]**
- sipIT = app + connected bottle + smartwatch drinking-gesture detection, with **lapse-contingent** text reminders: a 60-minute timer resets whenever the hourly goal is met; a reminder is sent only if the timer expires. Fluid goal 100 fl oz/day (about 2.96 L). — [sipIT trial protocol, Contemporary Clinical Trials 2024 (PMC10923155)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10923155)
- Protocol: 2-arm RCT, 216 planned, adults with a stone in the past 5 years and urine volume <= 2.0 L/day, primary outcome 24-h urine volume at 3 months, follow-up to 12 months, NIDDK-funded (R01DK124469). No results reported in the protocol. — [PMC10923155](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10923155)
- The protocol cites prior literature that usual care alone yields a mean urine output gain of about +0.35 L/day — a useful reference for how small "advice only" effects are. — [PMC10923155](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10923155)
- Earlier uncontrolled feasibility work: 3-month study in 31 patients, 83% felt sipIT helped them reach fluid goals; 1-month "mini sipIT" study, 85% felt it helped increase intake and 65% felt it helped reach goals **[secondary; self-perceived, no control group]**. — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03787615](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03787615); [Mini sipIT feasibility paper listing](https://www.em-consulte.com/article/1621088/feasibility-of-mini-sip-it-behavioral-intervention)

**Reminder app vs motivational bottle in healthy women (Reeves et al. 2023) [healthy adults, pilot, n=22, no control arm]**
- 22 healthy women aged 19-50 (mean 25.8 years; university staff/students) randomised to a 64-oz time-marked motivational bottle with self-recorded voice reminders (n=11) or a water-reminder tracker app (n=11) for 5 weeks, with a 30-day follow-up. 30 volunteered, 22 completed (27% attrition). — [Reeves et al., Journal of Exercise and Nutrition 2023;6(1):8](https://journalofexerciseandnutrition.com/index.php/JEN/article/download/142/122/480)
- Pooled self-reported intake rose from 55.85 +/- 18 oz/day (about 1.65 L) to 72.05 +/- 18.75 oz/day (about 2.13 L) at week 5, i.e. about +29% or roughly +16 oz (about 0.48 L); at the 30-day follow-up it was 69.65 +/- 20.65 oz (about 2.06 L), a decline of about 7% from the peak. — [Reeves et al. 2023](https://journalofexerciseandnutrition.com/index.php/JEN/article/download/142/122/480)
- By arm (oz/day, mean +/- SD): bottle 52.1 -> 66.6 -> 62.3; app 59.6 -> 77.5 -> 77.0 (baseline -> week 5 -> follow-up). No significant between-group difference at any time point. — [Reeves et al. 2023, Table 3](https://journalofexerciseandnutrition.com/index.php/JEN/article/download/142/122/480)
- Despite higher reported intake, urine markers did not improve: 41% (urine colour) and 45% (specific gravity) were still classified as dehydrated at week 5, and mean specific gravity rose slightly (1.010 -> 1.017). — [Reeves et al. 2023](https://journalofexerciseandnutrition.com/index.php/JEN/article/download/142/122/480)
- Drop-off: participants self-reported that they "did not frequently utilize their assigned hydration reminder tool during the 30-day follow-up period". — [Reeves et al. 2023](https://journalofexerciseandnutrition.com/index.php/JEN/article/download/142/122/480)
- Self-efficacy predicted intake at week 5 (R2 = 0.262, p = 0.03). Authors report no external funding. Limitations stated by authors: small sample, women only, visual dipstick reading. Additional limitations visible in the paper: intake is self-reported, no no-intervention control, weekly education infographics were also delivered to both arms, and some reported test statistics are internally inconsistent (e.g. "t(19) = -1.27, p = 0.96"). — [Reeves et al. 2023](https://journalofexerciseandnutrition.com/index.php/JEN/article/download/142/122/480)

**Workplace interventions [healthy adults; weak designs]**
- Taiwan smart-cup reminder study (Inquiry, 2022): baseline water intake at work was low — 586 +/- 318.5 ml (Company A) and 512 +/- 298.3 ml (Company B) — and intake at work increased significantly during the reminder period versus the non-reminder period **[secondary: search summary; I could not open the full text, so the size of the increase and the design details are unverified]**. — [Effectiveness of a Water Intake Program at the Workplace (PMC8998380)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8998380)
- "Hydroprompt" (arXiv 2016): a 3-week field deployment in work settings comparing three approaches — historical information, implicit (ambient) feedback of current hydration level, and explicit prompting when too much time has passed since the last drink. The abstract gives no sample size or effect sizes. — [Hydroprompt, arXiv:1603.01367](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.01367)

**Other**
- A 2024 RCT titled "Can Smartphone Applications Increase Water Intake?" (Kong & Chua, Journal of Exercise and Nutrition) exists but I could only see its citation, not its results **[secondary]**. — [search listing via World Nutrition Journal / DOAJ result set](https://doaj.org/article/91dc46213e5546929ac5085801b621d4)
- A registered single-blind RCT of a mobile hydration-tracking programme in older adults (NCT07043933, Türkiye) has no posted results. **[older adults]** — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT07043933](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07043933)

**Does more water improve outcomes at all? (context for health claims)**
- Systematic review of 18 RCTs of changed daily water intake (Hakam et al., JAMA Network Open 2024): sample sizes 14-631 (median 48), durations 4 days to 5 years; 10 studies (55%) had at least one significant positive result, 8 (44%) were entirely negative; no meta-analysis possible. — [Hakam et al. 2024 (PMC11589796)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11589796/)
- Benefits were clearest in specific groups: pre-meal water (about 500 ml before meals) and weight loss in adults with overweight (e.g. 1.3 kg more over 12 weeks, n=84, P=.03); stone recurrence 12/99 vs 27/100 over 5 years (Borghi, P=.008); recurrent UTI in women, 1.5 fewer episodes over 12 months with +1.5 L/day (Hooton, n=140). Headache: quality-of-life score improved but headache days did not. — [PMC11589796](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11589796/)
- In healthy adults one small trial (n=20, 2 weeks, 30 ml/kg/day) found a small rise in daytime blood pressure rather than a benefit; several outcomes (eGFR in CKD n=631, copeptin, etc.) were null. — [PMC11589796](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11589796/)
- Authors' caveats: limited quality and quantity (9 low risk of bias, 5 some concerns, 4 high risk), most outcomes tested in single small studies, no optimal daily intake established, intake should be individualised. Adherence was a problem even inside trials (e.g. adolescents averaged 1,135 ml/day against a 2,000 ml goal; dropout 21% vs 34% in one headache trial). — [PMC11589796](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11589796/)

### Inferences
- The realistic effect of a reminder/tracking tool over plain advice is on the order of a few hundred ml/day in the short term, mostly demonstrated in motivated patients with a medical reason to drink. Healthy office workers have weaker motivation, so HydraChat should not expect or promise larger effects.
- PUSH is the most important datapoint: even a far more intensive package than any consumer app (smart bottle + money + coaching + reminders) produced only a modest increase and adherence still "broke down". Reminders alone are unlikely to sustain behaviour; this argues for habit/context design rather than notification volume.
- PUSH's null result on recurrence concerns stone patients and a surrogate-to-outcome gap; it does not test whether mild under-drinkers feel better. It does, however, weaken any strong health-outcome claim an app might make.
- sipIT's lapse-contingent logic (remind only if the hourly goal was not met) is the closest published analogue to HydraChat's "stop reminding once the target is met" and "skip when already drinking" behaviour; it is designed on JITAI principles but its efficacy is not yet shown.
- Reeves 2023 illustrates a recurring measurement trap: self-reported intake rose but urine markers did not. In-app logged ml is not a validated outcome; logging itself may inflate numbers.

### Gaps
- I could not obtain the exact urine-volume difference, recurrence rates, hazard ratio or adherence curve from the PUSH Lancet paper (only press material); the writer should not quote numbers for PUSH beyond n=1,658 and "modest increase, no recurrence benefit".
- No published efficacy results found for the sipIT RCT (R01DK124469) as of this search.
- No adequately powered RCT of a consumer hydration app or push/SMS water reminders in healthy adult office workers was found. A widely repeated blog claim of "a 2020 JMIR study showing a 48% intake increase" could not be traced to a primary source and should not be used.
- Kong & Chua 2024 results and the Taiwan smart-cup study's effect size were not readable in this session.
- An Arizona State University thesis on desk water dispensers/bottles surfaced, but the figure in the search summary ("143 ounces/day") is implausible as stated and unverified; do not cite.
- No Vietnamese or Southeast-Asian office-worker hydration intervention trial was found (one Indonesian quasi-experimental app study, "Hidrasiku", surfaced by title only).

## 2. Which behaviour-change techniques have evidence for increasing water intake, and what do systematic reviews conclude?

### Takeaway
Reviews agree that most interventions raise water intake somewhat, with instruction plus self-monitoring the best-supported combination, but study quality is low-to-neutral, follow-up is short, and no review isolates the effect of prompts, habit stacking, environmental restructuring, social support or gamification for water specifically. Evidence for those techniques is borrowed from general habit and planning research.

### Cited Findings
- Chua et al. (Nephrology 2016;21(10):860-869) systematically reviewed non-pharmacological interventions to increase water intake in adults (search to March 2015). Interventions ranged from instruction alone to self-monitoring tools, providing water bottles, and counselling/education. Most increased intake; 13 studies reported an increase of at least 500 ml; none decreased intake. **The most effective strategies were instruction and self-monitoring using urine dipstick or 24-h urine volume.** Quality was "mostly neutral (63%) with no studies of high quality"; authors call for high-quality long-term studies. The review was framed for CKD trials. — [PubMed 26599520](https://www.pubmed.gov/26599520/); [DOI 10.1111/nep.12675 record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/nep.12675)
- Vargas-Garcia et al. (Obesity Reviews 2017): 40 controlled trials, 16,505 participants, on reducing sugar-sweetened beverages (SSB) or increasing water. A pooled water estimate was possible **only for children**: +80 ml/day (95% CI 6 to 155; 6 studies; P = 0.04) **[children]**. SSB reduction in adults was not significant: -13 ml/day (95% CI -44 to 18; 12 studies). For children, modelling/demonstrating the behaviour and home-based delivery were associated with larger effects. Interventions were mainly nutrition education/counselling. **[secondary: search summary of the abstract]** — [White Rose repository copy](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/119566/); [PubMed 28721697](https://www.pubmed.gov/28721697/)
- A conference abstract from the same group (29 studies) reported that techniques that "heightened motivation, enhanced planning, preparation and persistence" were more likely to yield lower SSB intake across populations **[secondary; conference abstract, about SSB not water]**. — [JECH conference abstract](https://jech.bmj.com/content/70/Suppl_1/A34.3.full.pdf)
- Self-monitoring by app plus goal: in the Reeves pilot, both a time-marked bottle (an environmental cue plus implicit goal markers) and a reminder/tracker app were associated with about +29% self-reported intake over 5 weeks, with no difference between tools; self-efficacy predicted intake. **[healthy women, n=22, no control]** — [Reeves et al. 2023](https://journalofexerciseandnutrition.com/index.php/JEN/article/download/142/122/480)
- Prompts/cues address a real barrier: 60% of stone formers named forgetting as the main limiting factor, and the smart-bottle arm reduced this (68.4% -> 45.4%). **[patients]** — [Stout et al. 2022 record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/a-randomized-trial-evaluating-the-use-of-a-smart-water-bottle-to-/)
- Financial incentives + goal setting + coaching + reminders + feedback (PUSH) produced only a modest urine-volume increase over two years. **[patients]** — [CHOP news release](https://www.chop.edu/news/behavioral-hydration-program-landmark-kidney-stone-prevention-trial-increased-urine-output)
- Habit formation: Lally et al. (2010) is consistently reported as finding a median of 66 days to reach automaticity with a range of about 18 to 254 days, in 96 volunteers, and that a single missed day did not materially derail habit formation while longer gaps (e.g. a week) could. Lally herself describes 66 days as a ballpark figure. **[secondary sources only in this session]** — [Inverse interview with Lally](https://www.inverse.com/article/62104-how-to-stick-to-new-years-resolutions)
- Structural barriers to drinking matter: in a water-intake trial in adolescents about 40% of the intervention group cited barriers such as not being able to drink in class. **[adolescents]** — [Hakam et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11589796/)

### Inferences
- Rough evidence ranking for water intake specifically: (1) instruction + self-monitoring — best supported, but low-quality studies; (2) prompts/reminders — supported short-term, mainly in patients; (3) environmental restructuring (bottle provided / on desk) — included in effective packages but never isolated; (4) goal setting and feedback — present in almost every effective package, not isolated; (5) social support/modelling — evidence only in children; (6) gamification, points, streaks — **no water-specific evidence found**.
- Lally's finding that one missed day does not harm habit formation is a direct argument against punitive, all-or-nothing streak resets.
- An app can only log and remind; the reviews suggest that pairing it with a physical cue (a bottle on the desk) is at least as plausible a driver as the notification itself, since the bottle arm performed like the app arm in Reeves 2023.
- Logging friction is the cost side of self-monitoring: sipIT's investment in semi-automated tracking (bottle + watch gestures) signals that researchers regard manual logging burden as a main adherence threat.

### Gaps
- No systematic review coding water-intake interventions with Michie's BCT Taxonomy v1 in adults was found; the question "which BCTs work for water" cannot be answered with pooled effect sizes.
- Implementation intentions: Gollwitzer & Sheeran's 2006 meta-analysis (94 studies, d = 0.65 on goal attainment; Advances in Experimental Social Psychology 38:69-119) is the standard reference, but I only saw it via blogs in this session **[not re-verified]**, and found no trial of if-then plans or habit stacking for water intake specifically.
- Lally 2010 (Eur J Soc Psychol, DOI 10.1002/ejsp.674) not opened directly **[not re-verified]**; note the study included a drinking behaviour among chosen habits per my recollection, but I could not confirm this.
- No trial isolating "bottle on desk"/water availability for adult office workers with a clean ml/day figure.
- Gardner's habit work and any trial of social support for adult water intake were not retrieved.

## 3. What is known about gamification and streaks in health apps (short vs long term, novelty decay, risks, over-consumption incentives)?

### Takeaway
Gamification has a small-to-medium short-term effect on a health behaviour (physical activity, g = 0.42) that shrinks to a very small effect at follow-up (g = 0.15), and none of this evidence is about water. Streaks motivate while intact but a visibly broken streak reduces subsequent engagement, especially when users blame themselves; I found no empirical study of streak anxiety in health apps and no evidence at all on over-drinking incentives in hydration apps.

### Cited Findings
- Mazeas et al. (JMIR 2022), meta-analysis of 16 RCTs, 2,407 participants: gamified interventions had a small-to-medium effect on physical activity, Hedges g = 0.42 (95% CI 0.14-0.69). — [JMIR 2022;24(1):e26779](https://www.jmir.org/2022/1/e26779)
- Same review: at follow-up (averaging 14 weeks after the intervention ended) the effect was weaker, g = 0.15 (95% CI 0.07-0.23); versus active (non-gamified) controls g = 0.23 (95% CI 0.05-0.41). The authors interpret persistence after follow-up as "not just a novelty effect" but call for rigorous confirmatory trials. **[secondary: figures from PEDro record/search summary; the JMIR page did not load]** — [PEDro record](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/69338); [JMIR article](https://www.jmir.org/2022/1/e26779)
- In cardiovascular patients, a 2025 JMIR Serious Games meta-analysis of 6 RCTs found a small short-term effect on physical activity (g = 0.32, 95% CI 0.19-0.45 after sensitivity analysis); the maintenance estimate rests on only 3 studies. **[patients; secondary]** — [JMIR Serious Games 2025;13:e64410](https://games.jmir.org/2025/1/e64410/PDF)
- Streaks: Silverman & Barasch, "On or Off Track: How (Broken) Streaks Affect Consumer Decisions", Journal of Consumer Research 2023;49(6):1095-1117. Across seven studies, a visibly intact streak increased later engagement relative to a visibly broken one, independent of actual past behaviour — it depends on how the log displays it. People treat keeping the logged streak as a goal in itself. The demotivating effect of a broken streak is stronger when people blame themselves rather than external causes and weaker when the streak can be repaired. **[consumer-behaviour experiments, not health trials; secondary summaries]** — [CU Boulder faculty research page](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions); [Psychology Today summary](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation)
- The same coverage notes participants sometimes engaged in costly behaviours to repair a streak, and a university write-up suggests apps may do better not to emphasise broken streaks in notifications — explicitly noted as not directly tested. — [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation); [Univ. of Delaware UDaily](https://udel.edu/udaily/2024/march/power-of-streaks-motivation-jackie-silverman)
- Financial incentives for meeting a fluid prescription (PUSH) did not produce more than a modest increase in urine output over two years. **[patients]** — [CHOP news release](https://www.chop.edu/news/behavioral-hydration-program-landmark-kidney-stone-prevention-trial-increased-urine-output)
- Claims about "streak anxiety", shame and all-or-nothing thinking, and remedies such as streak freezes or recovery paths, appear in designer blogs and parenting guides only — opinion, not controlled evidence. — [Yu-kai Chou blog](https://yukaichou.com/gamification-analysis/streak-design-gamification-motivation-burnout/); [ScreenWise guide](https://screenwiseapp.com/guides/duolingo-streaks-and-anxiety-in-kids)
- Physiological ceiling relevant to "winning" by drinking more: secondary sources put a healthy kidney's free-water excretion at roughly 0.8-1.0 L/hour, stress that the rate of intake matters more than the daily total, and note that most life-threatening water intoxication cases involved endurance exercise, military training or contests (e.g. a 2007 contest death after nearly 7.6 L). **[secondary/consumer health sources; primary case reports not retrieved]** — [Healthline](https://www.healthline.com/health/how-much-water-can-kill-you); [Thorne overview](https://thronescience.com/blogs/signals/hyponatremia-how-much-water-is-too-much)

### Inferences
- The drop from g = 0.42 to g = 0.15 is the best available quantitative picture of "gamification fades": expect points/streaks/cosmetic store to help most in the first weeks. This is from physical-activity trials; transfer to water is an assumption.
- Silverman & Barasch implies three concrete streak rules: (a) make streaks repairable (grace day / freeze), (b) attribute breaks to external causes rather than the user ("busy day" framing instead of "you failed"), (c) do not showcase a broken streak in a notification. The second and third are extrapolations of the moderators, not tested interventions.
- Because streak-keeping becomes "a goal in itself", a hydration streak can decouple from the health goal: users may log untruthfully or chug water late in the day to save a streak. No study documents this in hydration apps; it is a plausible risk, not an observed one.
- Rewarding volume above the daily target has no evidence of benefit (no optimal intake established — Hakam 2024) and a known, if rare, physiological downside when intake is fast. Points should therefore cap at the target and reward pacing (spread across the day) rather than volume. HydraChat's "stop reminding once the target is met" is consistent with this; the points system should apply the same cap.
- Crowding-out of intrinsic motivation by extrinsic rewards (overjustification) is a classic theoretical concern but I found no direct test in health apps in this search; state it as theory.

### Gaps
- **No evidence found, in either direction, on over-consumption incentives or hyponatraemia linked to hydration apps or gamified water tracking.** This is an absence of studies, not evidence of safety.
- No RCT isolating streaks (versus other game mechanics) in a health app; no empirical measurement of streak anxiety prevalence.
- Johnson et al. 2016 (Internet Interventions) and Cheng et al. 2019 gamification reviews, suggested in the brief, were not retrieved; their conclusions are not represented here.
- Subgroup numbers from Mazeas 2022 (e.g. g = 0.58 vs inactive controls) were garbled across summaries and are unverified.
- Primary sources for water-intoxication thresholds (IOM/National Academies DRI for water; exercise-associated hyponatraemia case series) were not retrieved; the 0.8-1.0 L/hour figure is from secondary pages.
- No evidence found on cosmetic stores/virtual goods in health apps.

## 4. Notification design: fatigue and habituation, frequency and timing, JITAI, deferral, varied vs fixed content, tone and persona

### Takeaway
Micro-randomised trials show a notification strongly raises the chance of opening an app in the next hour (about 3.5x) but does not delay disengagement, and effects of activity prompts decay within weeks (HeartSteps: +107% at the start, falling over 6 weeks). Evidence that varied or novel message content beats a fixed message is absent in the one trial that tested it; evidence that persona, humour or guilt tone improves adherence is essentially missing — chatbots improve liking and intention to use, not retention.

### Cited Findings

**Near-term effect vs long-term engagement (Drink Less MRT) [alcohol-reduction app users, UK]**
- Bell et al. (JMIR mHealth uHealth 2023): 30-day micro-randomised trial, 566 analysed (349 MRT, 120 standard daily notification, 97 no notification); target had been 2,000 so secondary analyses are under-powered. — [PMC10337295](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10337295)
- A notification raised the probability of opening the app in the following hour 3.5-fold (RR 3.52, 95% CI 2.91-4.25), and over 24 hours 1.26-fold (95% CI 1.19-1.34). — [PMC10337295](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10337295)
- **Varied vs fixed content:** a fixed standard message (RR 3.66, 2.99-4.48) and messages drawn from a new bank of 30 (RR 3.39, 2.77-4.13) performed the same. — [PMC10337295](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10337295)
- **Habituation:** the near-term effect did not significantly decay over 30 days (decay factor 0.993/day, 95% CI 0.975-1.012). A notification on the previous day reduced the standard message's effect by 11% (RR ratio 0.89, 0.60-1.31, not significant); the new-message effect was unchanged. — [PMC10337295](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10337295)
- **Disengagement was not prevented:** median time to disengagement (first of >= 7 consecutive days without use) was 11 days with standard notifications, 11 days in the MRT arm and 7 days with no notifications (log-rank P = .42); 68.2% (386/566) had disengaged within the 30 days. Median sessions over 30 days: 10.5, 9 and 6 respectively. — [PMC10337295](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10337295)
- Authors' conclusion: fixed or random schedules give a strong short-lived boost; notifications may work better as adaptive components, e.g. sent when a user shows signs of disengaging. — [PMC10337295](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10337295)

**Decay of contextually tailored prompts (HeartSteps MRT) [sedentary adults, n about 37-44, 6 weeks]**
- Klasnja et al. (Annals of Behavioral Medicine 2019): averaged over the study, a suggestion vs none increased the next-30-minute step count by 14% (p = .06), 35 extra steps over a 253-step average. Walking suggestions initially increased it by 107% (271 extra steps, p < .01), but the effect decreased over time. **Anti-sedentary suggestions had no detectable effect.** Suggestions were randomised at 5 decision points per day (morning commute, lunch, mid-afternoon, evening commute, after dinner). **[secondary: abstract via search summary]** — [PubMed 30192907](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30192907/)
- Methodological follow-ups on the same data describe the effect as deteriorating over time and note that habituation or burden would be expected to erode the causal effect. — [arXiv 2410.15049](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.15049); [arXiv 2004.10241](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.10241)

**Timing and tailoring**
- Bidargaddi et al. (2018) MRT in a workplace wellbeing app, 1,255 users over 89 days: a tailored health message made users 8.7% more likely to engage on weekends (95% CI 1.01-1.17) but only 2.5% more likely on weekdays (95% CI 0.98-1.07, not significant). **[secondary: search summary]** — [PMC6080195](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6080195)
- A review of MRTs for JITAIs notes repeated notifications can cause habituation (reduced responsiveness from frequent exposure) and positions MRTs as the way to find contexts where a prompt still works. **[secondary]** — [PMC7442945](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7442945)
- sipIT implements a JITAI-style rule for water: reminders are lapse-contingent (sent only when the 60-minute timer expires without the hourly goal being met). Efficacy not yet reported. **[patients]** — [PMC10923155](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10923155)

**HCI evidence on receptivity and deferral**
- Pielot et al., "Dismissed!" (MobileHCI 2018): 794,525 notifications from 278 users; a "surprisingly large fraction" arrive while the phone is unlocked or the corresponding app is in the foreground, suggesting optimisation for that case. — [Pielot MobileHCI18 PDF](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Pielot-MobileHCI18.pdf)
- Mehrotra et al., "My Phone and Me" (CHI 2016): 10,372 in-the-wild notifications and 474 questionnaire responses from 20 users. Receptivity depends on content type and sender; even notifications with important or useful content can be disruptive; alert modality significantly affects time to view; personality traits affect response time and perceived disruption. — [UCL Discovery copy of CHI16 paper](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1502364/1/CHI16.pdf)
- Related finding (Fischer et al., cited in the above literature): users react faster to notifications delivered right at the end of a task, e.g. after finishing a phone call. **[secondary]** — [UCL Discovery copy](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1502364/1/CHI16.pdf)
- Survey context: most respondents believed they receive roughly 20-50 or 50-100 notifications per day. **[secondary]** — [Pielot MobileHCI18 PDF](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Pielot-MobileHCI18.pdf)

**Tone, persona and chatbots**
- Meta-analysis of health conversational agents' communication competence (JMIR 2025): 31 studies (25 in meta-analysis), 4,525 participants; eleven communication strategies identified including humour, personalisation and self-disclosure; small-to-medium pooled effect on user outcomes such as likeability, intention to use and engagement. No humour-specific effect size was visible. **[secondary]** — [JMIR 2025;27:e76296 (PMC12582511)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12582511)
- Cevasco et al. (Journal of Medical Systems 2024), systematic review/meta-analysis of health chatbot RCTs 2016-2022: no effect of the chatbot arm on patient retention; unclear whether chatbots improve adherence. **[secondary]** — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10916-024-02059-x)

### Inferences
- Reminders reliably cause the next action when they arrive (strong hour-level effect), which fits water well because the target behaviour takes seconds. But the same trial shows they do not keep people in the app. HydraChat's retention must come from elsewhere (habit, low friction, a physical cue), not from notifying more.
- HeartSteps' null result for anti-sedentary prompts and decaying walking prompts is a caution for the stretch mini-quest: attaching a movement request to every reminder may be ignored and may add burden that accelerates habituation. Making the quest optional and occasional is the conservative reading.
- The Drink Less result (30 varied messages = 1 fixed message for near-term opening) directly challenges the assumption that AI-written varied copy improves response. Variety may still matter for liking/annoyance over months — untested. AI-generated variation should be justified as user experience, not as an evidenced efficacy feature, and it adds content-safety risk (see section 7).
- The chatbot evidence supports persona and humour for likeability and intention to use, not for adherence or retention. Personas (boss, crush, Gen Z friend, sprout) are a reasonable engagement bet with no outcome evidence; guilt- or pressure-toned personas have no supporting evidence and plausibly raise the self-blame that makes broken streaks more damaging (section 3).
- HydraChat's deferral rules (DND, landscape, chosen apps) and task-boundary delivery are consistent with Mehrotra/Fischer/Pielot (receptivity depends on context; delivery at breakpoints gets faster reaction), but those studies measured response time and perceived disruption, not health behaviour. Delivering when the phone is unlocked and idle is a supported heuristic.
- No source gives an "optimal number of reminders per day". The defensible rule is behaviour-contingent sending (only when behind pace, as in sipIT), a user-set cap, and stopping at target — all of which reduce exposure and thus habituation risk.
- Weekend vs weekday (Bidargaddi): prompts landed better on weekends; office-hours prompts compete with work. This supports quieter weekday defaults and aggressive deferral during focus periods.

### Gaps
- No dose-response study of notification frequency vs habituation or opt-out for health apps was found; no evidence-based "N per day".
- No study comparing humour, guilt, authority ("boss") or romantic/social ("crush") persona tones on adherence. No evidence on culturally specific tone for Vietnamese users.
- No study of floating chat-bubble/overlay delivery versus standard system notifications.
- Okoshi et al.'s breakpoint-based notification deferral results (CSCW 2017, "I'm Socializing!") surfaced only as a reference; effect sizes not retrieved. Nahum-Shani's JITAI framework paper was not opened directly.
- "Dismissed!" click-through rates (roughly 15-25% in a fragment) could not be confirmed and are not cited.
- No evidence on a 20-minute snooze interval specifically, nor on LLM-generated health nudges versus templated ones.
- The share of users who disable notifications over time was not found in the sources read.

## 5. Does pairing water breaks with micro-breaks or stretching have evidence (sedentary breaks, 20-20-20 rule)?

### Takeaway
Micro-breaks modestly improve vigour and fatigue (d about 0.35) and computer prompts reduce office sitting by about 12 minutes per workday, both with low-to-moderate certainty; the 20-20-20 eye rule has weak support, with the most direct trial negative. No study tests combining a drink prompt with a stretch, and no evidence was found for breaks as short as 10 seconds.

### Cited Findings
- Albulescu et al. (PLOS One 2022), meta-analysis of micro-breaks (breaks of up to 10 minutes): 22 samples, N = 2,335. Small significant effects on vigour (d = .36, p < .001; k = 9, n = 913) and fatigue (d = .35, p < .001; k = 9, n = 803); non-significant effect on overall performance (d = .16, p = .116; k = 15, n = 1,132). Performance benefits appeared only for less cognitively demanding tasks; longer breaks gave larger performance gains; recovery from highly depleting tasks may need more than 10 minutes. — [PubMed 36044424](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36044424/)
- Computer prompts to break up sitting in office workers (IJBNPA 2025), systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs: 18 studies, 1,164 office workers; objectively measured sitting fell by 12.46 min/workday (95% CI -18.12 to -6.80) and steps rose by 1,029.99/workday (95% CI 815.97 to 1,244). Ten studies used prompts alone, eight combined them with e.g. sit-stand desks; median duration 8 weeks (range 1-24). Work, musculoskeletal and cardiometabolic outcomes favoured prompts but were not significant. GRADE certainty low to moderate. — [PMC12164069](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12164069)
- In HeartSteps, phone-delivered anti-sedentary suggestions had no detectable effect on subsequent 30-minute step count. **[secondary]** — [PubMed 30192907](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30192907/)
- Taiwan workplace smart-cup study reported improvements alongside increased water intake (the search summary mentions sedentary time and blood pressure changes); details unverified. **[secondary, weak design]** — [PMC8998380](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8998380)
- 20-20-20 rule: a trial of 20-second scheduled breaks (Johnson & Rosenfield, Optometry and Vision Science 2023) concluded the results do not support them as a therapeutic intervention for digital eye strain; a critique is that participants were not told to focus on a specific distant detail. **[secondary: trade-press reports]** — [Review of Optometry](https://reviewofoptometry.com/article/202020-still-not-enough-to-alleviate-eye-strain); [Insight News](https://www.insightnews.com.au/does-the-20-20-20-rule-work-for-digital-eye-strain/)
- Systematic review of computer vision syndrome treatments (Singh et al., Ophthalmology 2022; 45 trials, 1991-2021): no high-certainty evidence for any commonly used therapy; several findings very low certainty. It did not specifically evaluate break schedules. **[secondary]** — [Eye on Optics summary](https://eyeonoptics.co.nz/live-articles/treating-computer-vision-syndrome-what-does-the-evidence-say)
- A paper titled "20-20-20 Rule: Are These Numbers Justified?" exists, questioning the specific numbers. **[title only]** — [BVS record](https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/ses/resource/pt/mdl-36473088)

### Inferences
- The combination is plausible and cheap — getting up to refill a bottle is itself a sitting break — but it is an untested bundle. Present the mini-quest as a pleasant add-on, not as an evidenced health intervention.
- A 10-second stretch is well below the break lengths studied in the micro-break literature (seconds to 10 minutes, with longer breaks working better). Expect at most a small wellbeing effect; do not claim productivity, posture or eye-health benefits.
- If an eye-rest prompt is included, the copy should avoid asserting that 20-20-20 "prevents eye strain"; "look away from the screen for a moment" is defensible, the specific rule is not.
- Hourly-ish water reminders line up with the 30-60 minute sitting-break cadence used in prompt studies, so a single combined prompt avoids doubling the notification load.

### Gaps
- No study of drink + stretch pairing; no study of 10-second movement "snacks" as a prompt add-on.
- The Albulescu paper's exact definition range for micro-breaks and the Johnson & Rosenfield trial's sample size and effect numbers were not read in the primary sources.
- A blog claim of a 2023 trial showing 50% symptom reduction with 20-20-20 could not be verified and is excluded.
- No evidence found on whether extra water intake itself increases standing/walking via toilet or refill trips in office workers.

## 6. Typical retention of health and habit apps, and design features associated with sustained use

### Takeaway
Retention is very low by default: in an objective panel study of 93 mental-health apps, median day-30 retention was 3.3% (day-15 3.9%), and in a behaviour-change app trial 68% of users disengaged within 30 days (median 7-11 days). Vendor benchmarks for Health & Fitness put day-1 at about 20-28%, day-7 at 7-18% and day-30 at 3-8%; trackers and peer-support apps retain somewhat better than content-only apps.

### Cited Findings
- Baumel et al. (JMIR 2019;21(9):e14567): 93 Android mental-health apps with >= 10,000 installs, SimilarWeb panel data Aug 2017-Jul 2018. Median retention among users who opened the app on day 0: day 1 69.4% (IQR 27.8%), day 15 3.9% (IQR 10.3%), day 30 3.3% (IQR 6.2%; available for 59 apps). The drop is sharpest between day 1 and day 10. Median daily open rate 4.0% (IQR 4.7%). — [PMC6785720](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6785720)
- By app type at day 30: peer support 8.9% (only 2 apps), tracker apps 6.1% (IQR 20.4%), mindfulness/meditation 4.7%, breathing-exercise apps 0.0%. Tracker apps also had a higher daily open rate (median 6.3%) than breathing apps (1.6%). — [PMC6785720](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6785720)
- Limitations: Android only, anonymous panel (no user characteristics), nothing beyond day 30, small subgroups. Note that the day-1 figure here is relative to users who opened on day 0 and is not comparable to vendor "D1 retention". — [PMC6785720](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6785720)
- Drink Less trial: 68.2% (386/566) of new users disengaged within the 30-day window; median time to disengagement 7 days without notifications and 11 days with them (difference not significant). — [PMC10337295](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10337295)
- Even hydration tools decay quickly in research settings: participants reported not frequently using their reminder tool in the 30 days after a 5-week study. — [Reeves et al. 2023](https://journalofexerciseandnutrition.com/index.php/JEN/article/download/142/122/480)
- **Industry benchmarks (secondary, vendor-published, inconsistent methods; directional only):**
  - OneSignal 2024, Health & Fitness: day 1 28%, day 7 18.13%, day 30 8.48% (all-category about 28% / 17.9% / 7.9%). — [GetStream retention guide citing OneSignal](https://getstream.io/blog/app-retention-guide)
  - Sonar, Health & Fitness: 27% / 13% / 8%. — [Sonar benchmark post](https://trysonar.app/blog/app-user-retention-benchmarks-and-what-moves-it)
  - Other ranges for Health & Fitness: about 20-27% day 1, about 7% day 7, about 3% day 30. — [Lovable guide](https://lovable.dev/guides/what-is-a-good-retention-rate-for-an-app)
  - Braze day-30 for Health & Fitness: 3.9% iOS, 3.4% Android. — [Braze](https://www.braze.com/resources/articles/mobile-app-retention)
  - Android overall about 21% day 1 and 2.1% day 30, below iOS. — [Phiture](https://phiture.com/mobilegrowthstack/managing-retention-rate-benchmarks-and-expectations/)
  - Adjust cross-industry averages about 26% / 13% / 7%. — [Adjust](https://www.adjust.com/blog/what-makes-a-good-retention-rate/)

### Inferences
- A realistic planning baseline for an Android habit app is day-30 retention in the low single digits; anything above about 8% at day 30 would be at the top of published vendor ranges. Success metrics should be set against that, not against intuition.
- HydraChat is a tracker with reminders, which is the better-retaining category in Baumel's data (6.1% vs 3.3% median at day 30) — but still means roughly 94% gone in a month.
- Because the steepest loss is in the first 10 days, onboarding and the first week (goal set-up, first cue pairing, low logging friction) matter more than long-run mechanics such as the cosmetic store.
- An alternative reading of low retention (raised in commentary on Baumel) is that some users leave because the need is met. For a water habit this is a legitimate success state: a user who drinks regularly without the app has succeeded. Retention is therefore an imperfect proxy for benefit.
- Adaptive re-engagement (notify when a user shows signs of lapsing rather than on a fixed schedule) is the authors' suggested direction in Drink Less; it is a hypothesis, not a demonstrated effect.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed day-1/7/30 benchmark specific to hydration or habit-tracker apps was found; no Android-only Health & Fitness benchmark for 2025-2026 from a primary report (AppsFlyer/Adjust reports not opened directly).
- I did not retrieve systematic reviews on design features predicting sustained engagement (e.g. personalisation, reminders, social features, human support), so "features associated with sustained use" rests only on Baumel's app-type comparison and the Drink Less trial.
- No retention data for apps using overlay bubbles or persona chat, and none for the Vietnamese market.

## 7. What should such an app avoid?

### Takeaway
The evidence supports avoiding four things: rewards for intake beyond the target (no proven benefit of more water in healthy adults, and rapid over-drinking is physiologically risky), self-blaming or shaming feedback around missed days (broken streaks demotivate most when users blame themselves), high-frequency fixed-schedule notifications (they do not prevent disengagement and prompt effects decay), and health claims that outrun the trials. All four are inferences from adjacent evidence; none has been tested in a hydration app.

### Cited Findings
- No optimal daily water intake is established by RCTs and benefits are concentrated in specific groups (stone formers, recurrent UTI, pre-meal water for weight loss); evidence is limited in quality and quantity, with 8 of 18 trials entirely negative. — [Hakam et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11589796/)
- In healthy adults, one small trial of 30 ml/kg/day showed a small rise in daytime blood pressure rather than a benefit. — [Hakam et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11589796/)
- Even in the patient group with the strongest rationale, an intensive adherence programme raised urine output modestly and did not reduce stone recurrence (PUSH, n=1,658). — [CHOP news release](https://www.chop.edu/news/behavioral-hydration-program-landmark-kidney-stone-prevention-trial-increased-urine-output)
- Reported intake can rise without measurable hydration improvement (41-45% still classified dehydrated by urine markers after 5 weeks of higher self-reported intake). — [Reeves et al. 2023](https://journalofexerciseandnutrition.com/index.php/JEN/article/download/142/122/480)
- Kidney free-water excretion is roughly 0.8-1.0 L/hour in healthy adults per secondary sources, and rate of intake matters more than daily total; documented severe cases cluster around contests and endurance/military settings. **[secondary]** — [Healthline](https://www.healthline.com/health/how-much-water-can-kill-you); [Thorne overview](https://thronescience.com/blogs/signals/hyponatremia-how-much-water-is-too-much)
- sipIT's trial excluded people with conditions that preclude high fluid intake, i.e. a universal "drink more" target is not appropriate for everyone. **[patients]** — [PMC10923155](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10923155)
- A visibly broken streak reduces subsequent engagement, more so under self-blame, less so when repairable. — [CU Boulder research page](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions)
- Fixed or random daily notifications gave a short-term open-rate boost but no difference in time to disengagement (P = .42). — [PMC10337295](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10337295)
- Prompt effects decline over weeks (HeartSteps), and repeated notifications risk habituation. — [PubMed 30192907](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30192907/); [PMC7442945](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7442945)
- Even useful notifications are disruptive at the wrong moment. — [Mehrotra et al. CHI 2016](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1502364/1/CHI16.pdf)
- The 20-20-20 rule lacks supporting trial evidence; micro-breaks have small effects on vigour/fatigue and no significant effect on overall performance. — [Review of Optometry](https://reviewofoptometry.com/article/202020-still-not-enough-to-alleviate-eye-strain); [PubMed 36044424](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36044424/)
- Manufacturer-hosted summaries present trial results more favourably than the abstract warrants (the HidrateSpark blog omits the wide confidence intervals and the 40% missing follow-up). — [HidrateSpark blog](https://hidratespark.com/blogs/hidrate-spark/use-of-hidratespark-2-0-improves-fluid-intake-levels-for-study-participants-who-form-kidney-stones); [UMN Experts record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/a-randomized-trial-evaluating-the-use-of-a-smart-water-bottle-to-/)

### Inferences
Avoid (each mapped to HydraChat's feature set):
- **Rewarding excess.** No points, streak bonuses, leaderboards or store currency for ml above the daily target; no "biggest drinker" mechanics. Consider a soft ceiling/pace check (e.g. flag very large amounts logged in a short window) since the risk is tied to rate.
- **One-size targets presented as medical advice.** Offer an adjustable target with a note that people with kidney, heart or liver conditions or on fluid restriction should follow their clinician; do not present a fixed litres figure as a requirement.
- **Shaming and guilt personas.** Keep "boss"/"crush" personas playful rather than disappointed or guilt-inducing; do not notify about a broken streak; provide streak repair/grace days. Persona tone has no adherence evidence either way, so the downside risk (self-blame) is the deciding factor.
- **Notification volume as the retention lever.** No fixed high-frequency schedule; send only when behind pace, cap per day, keep the deferral rules, stop at target (already designed). Do not escalate frequency for lapsing users.
- **Mandatory add-ons.** The stretch mini-quest should never gate logging water or earning the basic reward; anti-sedentary prompts had no detectable effect in HeartSteps and add burden.
- **Unverifiable claims in copy and in AI-generated messages.** Do not claim water intake improves productivity, skin, weight, detox, headache or kidney health for healthy users; do not claim the stretch or eye rest prevents strain. Because messages are AI-written, constrain the model so personas cannot generate health claims or encourage drinking more than the target.
- **Treating logged ml as proof of benefit.** Self-reported intake is not a validated hydration measure; avoid "you are now hydrated" verdicts.
- **Over-reliance on novelty mechanics.** Gamification's effect shrinks after the intervention period (g 0.42 -> 0.15 in physical activity); the cosmetic store should not be the main retention plan.

### Gaps
- None of these "avoid" items has been tested as a design variable in a hydration app; they are extrapolations from adjacent trials and consumer-behaviour experiments.
- No regulatory analysis (Google Play health-claims policy, Vietnamese advertising rules for health statements) was done here; that needs a separate source.
- No data on how often consumer hydration apps actually reward over-target intake or on any resulting harm.
- No evidence on safety or accuracy of LLM-generated health nudges was retrieved.
