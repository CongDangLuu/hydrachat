## 1. Do coffee, tea, milk, juice, soup, coconut water, sports drinks and beer count toward daily fluid intake? (Beverage Hydration Index)

### Takeaway
In the only large BHI trial (Maughan et al. 2016), 9 of 12 test drinks, including coffee, hot and cold tea, cola, diet cola, sports drink, sparkling water and 4% lager, produced the same 4-hour urine output as still water; only ORS, full-fat milk and skimmed milk (and orange juice at 2-3 h) were retained better. For an app, essentially all common non-alcoholic drinks can be counted at roughly 1:1 with water for hydration; the reason to treat them differently is sugar/energy and caffeine load, not fluid loss.

### Cited Findings
- Design **[read in full text]**: 72 euhydrated, fasted, recreationally active males (age 25 (4) y, 77.3 (9.9) kg), three UK labs; each drank 1 L of still water plus three of 12 other drinks over 30 min (4 equal portions 7.5 min apart); urine collected for 4 h; n = 17 observations per test drink (16 for orange juice and diet cola, 15 for tea). — [Maughan et al. 2016, AJCN, accepted manuscript](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/12468/1/Maughan%20et%20al%20AJCN%20Beverage%20hydration%20index%20final%20accepted%20manuscript%20with%20DOI%20NPW%20edits.pdf)
- BHI definition **[read in full text]**: each individual's cumulative urine mass after still water divided by cumulative urine mass after the test drink; still water = 1.0; reported at 2 h. Repeatability of BHI: coefficient of variation 18% (~180 ml), so differences smaller than about 2 x CV are not meaningful. — [Maughan et al. 2016](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/12468)
- 4-h urine mass, mean (SD) **[read in full text]**: still water 1337 (330) g; ORS 1038 (333) g; full-fat milk 1052 (267) g; skimmed milk 1049 (334) g (all P < 0.001 vs water). Cola, diet cola, hot tea, cold tea, coffee, lager, orange juice, sparkling water and sports drink were not different from water at 4 h. — [Maughan et al. 2016](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/12468)
- BHI at 2 h stated numerically in the text **[read in full text]**: skimmed milk 1.58 (0.60); ORS 1.54 (0.74); full-fat milk 1.50 (0.58). Mean difference vs water for orange juice 0.39 (95% CI 0.05 to 0.73), i.e. BHI about 1.39. — [Maughan et al. 2016](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/12468)
- BHI for the other drinks is given only as a bar chart (Figure 2), not as numbers in the text. My visual reading of the bars (approximate, +/- 0.05, **not author-stated values**): sparkling water ~1.0; cola ~1.2; diet cola ~1.1; sports drink ~1.0; lager ~1.0; coffee ~0.9; hot tea ~1.0; cold tea ~1.1. None of these differed significantly from water and all are inside the 2 x CV band. — [Maughan et al. 2016, Figure 2](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/12468/1/Maughan%20et%20al%20AJCN%20Beverage%20hydration%20index%20final%20accepted%20manuscript%20with%20DOI%20NPW%20edits.pdf)
- After correcting for the drinks' water content (88% to 100%), ORS, full-fat milk and skimmed milk remained higher than water (mean differences 0.50, 0.32, 0.44) but orange juice no longer did (P = 0.11). — [Maughan et al. 2016](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/12468)
- Drink composition (Table 2) **[read in full text]**, per litre: coffee (Nescafe Original, black) 212 mg caffeine, 99% water; hot and cold black tea 179 mg caffeine; cola 96 mg caffeine and 10.6 g carbohydrate/100 ml (420 kcal/L); diet cola 127 mg caffeine; orange juice 10.5 g carbohydrate/100 ml (470 kcal/L), K 33 mmol/L; lager 330 kcal/L, 94% water; sports drink (Powerade) 3.9 g/100 ml, Na 21 mmol/L; ORS (Dioralyte) Na 55, K 20 mmol/L; full-fat milk 640 kcal/L, Na 18, K 41 mmol/L, 88% water; skimmed milk 350 kcal/L, Na 19, K 40 mmol/L. — [Maughan et al. 2016, Table 2](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/12468/1/Maughan%20et%20al%20AJCN%20Beverage%20hydration%20index%20final%20accepted%20manuscript%20with%20DOI%20NPW%20edits.pdf)
- Authors' explanation: drinks with the highest macronutrient and electrolyte content were retained best (slower gastric emptying, sodium/potassium retention); caffeine at 96-212 mg per litre had no effect on net fluid balance. — [Maughan et al. 2016](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/12468)
- **Funding/conflict flag** **[read in full text]**: funded by a grant from the European Hydration Institute (EHI); Maughan chaired the EHI Scientific Advisory Board; Watson had received EHI funding. EHI was founded with Coca-Cola support (the latter is my background knowledge, not verified in this session). The authors also note the BHI could be used by industry "to label products". — [Maughan et al. 2016](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/12468)
- Limitations stated or evident **[read in full text]**: males only, age 18-35; single 1 L bolus at rest after an overnight fast; only 4 h follow-up; six participants withdrew after vomiting on the tea trial; commercial drinks so individual ingredients could not be isolated. — [Maughan et al. 2016](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/12468)
- Number discrepancy: an EHI dossier quotes 1.53 (ORS), 1.32 (full-fat milk), 1.44 (skimmed milk). These are close to the water-content-corrected values in the paper, not a different result; the published abstract values are 1.54 / 1.50 / 1.58. — [EHI dossier](https://cieah.ulpgc.es/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/ehi-hydration-index-dossier.pdf); [Maughan et al. 2016](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/12468)
- Longer time scale: the authors cite Grandjean et al. (no difference in 24-h hydration status across combinations of water, caffeinated cola and coffee) and Tucker et al. (24-h hydration not different with water only versus a variety of drinks, provided total volume was adequate). — [Maughan et al. 2016](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/12468)
- Follow-up, Sollanek et al. 2018 (AJCN): neither body mass nor sex influenced BHI outcomes when comparing three commercial beverages **[title/abstract only]**. — [search record via Millard-Stafford et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8465972/)
- Follow-up, Millard-Stafford et al. 2021 (Nutrients), 19 healthy young adults at rest: BHI did not differ among electrolyte-only, carbohydrate-electrolyte and dipeptide-electrolyte drinks; adding electrolytes in the sports-drink range did not consistently improve BHI versus water; electrolyte content appears to make the largest contribution **[abstract only]**. — [Millard-Stafford et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8465972/)
- Follow-up from the Maughan group (title only): "Sucrose and sodium but not caffeine content influence the retention of beverages in humans under euhydrated conditions". — [Maughan et al. 2018/2019 manuscript](https://research.bangor.ac.uk/portal/files/20464471/2018_Sucrose_and_sodium.pdf/1000)
- Coconut water: Kalman et al. 2012 (JISSN), 12 exercise-trained men after a 60-min dehydrating treadmill bout; coconut water, coconut water from concentrate, bottled water and a sports drink all promoted rehydration with no clear winner; more bloating and stomach upset reported with coconut water. Funded by VitaCoco **[abstract/secondary only]**. — [Kalman et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3293068/); [NutraIngredients coverage](https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2012/02/06/Coconut-water-matches-carb-electrolyte-sports-beverage-for-rehydration-Study)
- Popkin et al. 2010 treat all beverages plus food moisture as sources of total water intake; fruit juice and fat-free milk are listed at 80-99% water. — [Popkin, D'Anci & Rosenberg 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2908954/)

### Inferences
- A simple, defensible app rule: count water, sparkling water, unsweetened tea (trà đá), black coffee, milk, soy milk, juice, coconut water, broth/soup and soft drinks at 100% of volume toward the fluid goal. A "hydration multiplier" finer than 0.9-1.0 for coffee/tea is not supported by the data, because the measured differences are within the 18% repeatability noise.
- Milk and ORS scoring ~1.5 does not mean "milk is 1.5x better" in daily life: BHI is a 2-h retention ratio after a 1 L bolus, and the kidneys of a sedentary euhydrated person will excrete surplus either way. Do not give bonus credit above 1.0.
- BHI measures retention, not health value. A cola scoring ~1.2 is "hydrating" but carries ~37 g sugar per 350 ml (10.6 g/100 ml x 350 ml), which the app should surface separately (see section 3).
- No BHI study covers the drinks Vietnamese office workers actually use (cà phê sữa đá with condensed milk, trà sữa, nước mía, trà đá, sữa đậu nành, nước dừa at rest). Values must be extrapolated from composition: all are >85% water, so they count; sweetened ones carry a sugar caveat.

### Gaps
- No BHI data for soup/broth, soy milk, sugarcane juice, milk tea, condensed-milk coffee or coconut water at rest. Coconut water evidence is post-exercise only and manufacturer-funded.
- No BHI data in women in the original trial, in older adults (one later study exists but was not read), or in hot-humid conditions.
- Per-drink BHI numbers for 8 of the 12 drinks are only available as a chart; the journal version may have a supplementary table I could not access.

## 2. Caffeine and diuresis: dose threshold, habitual intake, and Vietnamese coffee / trà đá

### Takeaway
A measurable diuretic effect appears only with a large acute dose (about 250-300 mg or more, taken at once, mainly in people not used to caffeine), and even then the extra urine is small (about +109 ml in a meta-analysis at a median 300 mg). Habitual moderate coffee or tea intake does not cause net fluid loss; the water in the cup more than covers it.

### Cited Findings
- Maughan & Griffin 2003 review (J Hum Nutr Diet 16(6):411-420): acute ingestion of caffeine in large doses (at least 250-300 mg, equivalent to 2-3 cups of coffee or 5-8 cups of tea) causes a short-term stimulation of urine output in people deprived of caffeine for days or weeks; tolerance develops with regular use; the most ecologically valid studies do not support fluid loss beyond the volume ingested with normal habitual intake **[abstract only, hosted on a coffee-industry site]**. — [Maughan & Griffin 2003 summary](https://www.aboutcoffee.org/health/research/caffeine-ingestion-and-fluid-balance-a-review/)
- Maughan et al. 2016 restate the threshold **[read in full text]**: "An acute dose of less than 250-300 mg of caffeine is unlikely to have a measurable effect on urine output, though such an effect is likely to be seen when the dose exceeds about 300 mg"; no impact of 96-212 mg caffeine on net fluid balance in their trial. — [Maughan et al. 2016](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/12468)
- Killer, Blannin & Jeukendrup 2014 (PLoS One): 50 male habitual coffee drinkers, counterbalanced crossover, 3 days each of 4 x 200 ml coffee per day (4 mg/kg caffeine per day) versus the same volume of water, with diet and activity controlled. Total body water by deuterium dilution: 51.5 +/- 1.4 vs 51.4 +/- 1.3 kg; 24-h urine volume 2409 +/- 660 vs 2428 +/- 669 ml; no differences in urine specific gravity, osmolality or blood markers; urinary sodium excretion higher with coffee (P = 0.02). Conclusion: coffee in moderation hydrates similarly to water in caffeine-habituated males **[abstract only]**. — [Killer et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3886980)
- Killer 2014 limitations: men only, habituated drinkers only, free-living not metabolic ward, no decaffeinated control. The trade press headline "Four cups of Nescafé a day" suggests an industry link; I could not verify the funding statement from the paper itself. — [NutraIngredients coverage](https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2014/01/10/Four-cups-of-Nescafe-a-day-keeps-dehydration-at-bay/)
- Zhang et al. 2015 meta-analysis (J Sci Med Sport): 16 studies, 28 effect sizes, median caffeine dose 300 mg; overall effect size 0.29, equal to +109 +/- 195 ml urine or +16.0 +/- 19.2% versus no caffeine. Effect at rest ES 0.54; during exercise ES 0.10 (not significant). Larger in women (ES 0.75) than men (ES 0.13). Authors' conclusion: concerns about unwanted fluid loss are unwarranted, particularly around exercise **[abstract only]**. — [Zhang et al. 2015](https://scholars.nova.edu/en/publications/caffeine-and-diuresis-during-rest-and-exercise-a-meta-analysis/); [PubMed 25154702](https://www.pubmed.gov/25154702/)
- Vietnam-specific caffeine content: robusta is Vietnam's main coffee species and its beans contain about 2.2-2.7% caffeine (pharmacy-chain health article, not a primary source). — [Nhà thuốc Long Châu](https://nhathuoclongchau.com.vn/bai-viet/ca-phe-robusta-la-gi-nguon-goc-cong-dung-va-nhung-dieu-can-biet.html)
- Vietnamese retail coffee is highly variable: in a Vinatas survey of 253 samples from Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City, Binh Duong and Soc Trang, 30.04% had very low caffeine (under 1 g per litre) and 5 samples contained none (adulterated product) **[news report]**. — [VOV](https://amp.vov.vn/en/economy/vietnamese-served-low-quality-coffee-330203.vov)

### Inferences
- The +109 ml figure at ~300 mg means a strong coffee still delivers net positive fluid whenever the cup holds more than about 100 ml; the risk case is a very small, very strong serving (a 60-100 ml phin shot of robusta) taken by someone who rarely drinks coffee. Even then the net effect is roughly neutral rather than dehydrating.
- Zhang's subgroup result (larger effect at rest and in women) matters for this app's users, who are sedentary and include women; but the absolute volume is still small, and it is a subgroup finding from a small set of studies.
- Trà đá is very dilute tea; Maughan's full-strength black tea (179 mg caffeine per litre) was indistinguishable from water, so trà đá can be counted 1:1 with water.
- Reasonable app treatment: count coffee and tea at full volume; do not subtract. The useful caffeine-related feature is a daily caffeine tally or late-afternoon sleep warning, not a hydration penalty. A common safety ceiling of 400 mg/day is quoted in Vietnamese consumer health articles ([Long Châu](https://nhathuoclongchau.com.vn/bai-viet/lieu-luong-caffeine-tieu-thu-hang-ngay-cua-ban-co-vuot-qua-muc-khuyen-nghi-khong.html)); I did not verify it against EFSA/FDA primary documents in this session.
- For cà phê sữa đá the bigger issue is condensed-milk sugar, not diuresis (see section 3).

### Gaps
- I found no study that measured caffeine in mg per served cup of Vietnamese phin coffee, cà phê sữa đá or trà đá. A VJST article comparing caffeine in tea, coffee and energy drinks on the Vietnamese market appeared in search results (b.vjst.vn) but could not be opened; the writer should not quote a mg-per-cup figure.
- No trial of caffeine and fluid balance in a hot-humid climate or in an Asian population was found.
- Maughan & Griffin 2003 and Zhang 2015 were only seen as abstracts; exact wording of conclusions should be treated as paraphrase.

## 3. Sugary drinks and milk tea: hydration value versus health cost, and how an app should treat them

### Takeaway
Sugar-sweetened drinks hydrate about as well as water in the short term (cola and orange juice were not worse than water in the BHI trial), but a single can exceeds WHO's conditional free-sugars target of about 25 g/day. The evidence-based position is "counts as fluid, but is not a good way to hydrate"; the clearest benefit seen in trials comes from replacing caloric drinks with water.

### Cited Findings
- WHO 2015 guideline: adults and children should reduce free sugars to less than 10% of total energy intake (strong recommendation); a further reduction to below 5%, roughly 25 g (6 teaspoons) per day, is a conditional recommendation. Free sugars include added sugars plus sugars in honey, syrups and fruit juices, but not sugars in whole fruit, vegetables or milk. — [WHO news release, 4 March 2015](https://who.int/news/item/04-03-2015-who-calls-on-countries-to-reduce-sugars-intake-among-adults-and-children)
- Regular cola in the BHI trial contained 10.6 g carbohydrate per 100 ml (420 kcal/L); orange juice 10.5 g per 100 ml (470 kcal/L); both were retained at least as well as water. — [Maughan et al. 2016, Table 2](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/12468/1/Maughan%20et%20al%20AJCN%20Beverage%20hydration%20index%20final%20accepted%20manuscript%20with%20DOI%20NPW%20edits.pdf)
- Replacing sugar-sweetened beverages, juice and milk with water is linked with reduced energy intake, by about 10-13% of total energy intake; "there is very minimal evidence on the effects of just adding water to the diet". — [Popkin et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2908954/)
- 2024 meta-analysis of 8 RCTs (Nutrients): substituting water for sugar-sweetened drinks gave -0.81 kg (95% CI -1.66 to 0.03, I2 = 2%), not statistically significant; authors say replacement "might offer a modest benefit" **[abstract only]**. — [PubMed 38612997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38612997/)
- Vietnam: sugary-drink consumption has risen quickly over the past 10 years; an estimate quoted in Vietnamese press is about 1 litre of sugary drinks per person per week (method not stated in the article). — [Báo Lào Cai](https://baolaocai.vn/trung-binh-mot-nguoi-viet-tieu-thu-1-lit-nuoc-ngot-moi-tuan-post382411.html)
- Vietnam's National Institute of Nutrition (deputy director Trương Tuyết Mai) describes regular sugary-drink consumption as a cause of overweight, obesity and metabolic disorders in adults and children **[news report of expert statement]**. — [VnExpress](https://vnexpress.net/do-uong-co-duong-gay-thua-can-beo-phi-4856331.html)

### Inferences
- Arithmetic from the cited composition: a 350 ml cup of regular cola carries about 37 g sugar (10.6 g x 3.5), about 1.5 times the WHO conditional 25 g/day target, and about 150 kcal. A 350 ml orange juice is similar (about 37 g, and juice sugars count as free sugars under the WHO definition).
- Suggested app treatment: (a) count sugary drinks at full volume for the hydration total, because subtracting would be physiologically wrong and would teach a myth; (b) tag them and show a separate sugar note or soft daily "sweet drinks" counter; (c) frame nudges as substitution ("swap one trà sữa for water or trà đá không đường") since substitution is where trial evidence exists.
- Avoid "sugary drinks dehydrate you" messaging; it is not supported by the BHI data.
- Milk's sugars (lactose) are outside the WHO free-sugars definition, so plain milk is not a "sugary drink" for this purpose; sweetened condensed milk in cà phê sữa đá is added sugar.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve primary dose-response data for sugar-sweetened beverages and type 2 diabetes, weight gain or cardiovascular risk (e.g. Imamura et al. BMJ 2015, Malik et al.); the writer should cite those from another researcher's notes or treat the direction of effect as established but unquantified here.
- No measured sugar content for Vietnamese milk tea (trà sữa), cà phê sữa đá or nước mía was found; do not quote grams per cup.
- No reliable Vietnamese national survey of beverage volumes per day in adults was found; the "1 litre per week" figure has no stated method. Vietnam's excise tax on sugary drinks (planned around 2027) is background knowledge I did not verify.
- The WHO 2023 guideline on non-sugar sweeteners (relevant to "diet" drinks) was not researched.

## 4. Alcohol: at what strength does a drink stop being net hydrating?

### Takeaway
Weak beer (up to about 2% alcohol) behaves like a non-alcoholic drink; around 4% a small extra diuresis appears (about 12% more urine than alcohol-free beer) but the drink is still net positive for fluid; no study I found pins an exact crossover strength, and stronger drinks (wine, spirits) are assumed, not shown here, to be net dehydrating.

### Cited Findings
- 4% lager (Carling), 1 L, euhydrated men: 4-h urine output not different from water; BHI about 1.0 (chart reading). The authors suggest alcohol may have cancelled the retention benefit expected from the lager's energy content (330 kcal/L). — [Maughan et al. 2016](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/12468)
- Hobson & Maughan 2010, as cited by Maughan 2016: 12% greater diuresis after 1 L of 4% lager versus the same volume of non-alcoholic beer, in euhydrated subjects; described as the only study of alcohol and fluid balance in a euhydrated state at that time. — [Maughan et al. 2016](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/12468)
- Hobson & Maughan 2010 detail **[blog summary of the paper, low reliability]**: urine about 1279 ml with alcoholic vs 1121 ml with non-alcoholic beer when euhydrated; about 261 vs 174 ml when hypohydrated (P = 0.057), i.e. alcohol's diuretic action is blunted when the body is already short of water. — [Alcohol Reports summary](https://alcoholreports.blogspot.com/2010/05/hydration-status-and-diuretic-action-of.html)
- Shirreffs & Maughan 1997 (post-exercise rehydration with 0, 1, 2 and 4% alcohol drinks), as described in a later paper: only at the highest concentration (4%) was there a small detrimental effect on fluid retention **[secondary description]**. — [Wijnen et al. 2016, Frontiers in Nutrition](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5066341)
- Wijnen et al. 2016: post-exercise drinks equal to 100% of sweat loss; after 1 h, urine output was higher with 5% beer than with an isotonic sports drink (299 +/- 143 vs 105 +/- 67 ml, P < 0.01); none of the drinks (0%, 2%, 5% beer, water, sports drink) achieved full rehydration **[search summary]**. — [Wijnen et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5066341)

### Inferences
- Practical tiers for an app: alcohol-free and low-alcohol beer (up to ~2%): count as fluid. Regular beer (~4-5%): fluid-neutral to slightly positive, but should not be promoted; simplest is to count it at a reduced fraction or not at all. Wine and spirits: do not count.
- Because the app is a health nudge, a conservative product choice is to allow logging alcohol for awareness but credit 0 ml toward the goal and suggest a glass of water alongside. This is a product/health-messaging decision, not something the fluid-balance data strictly require for beer.

### Gaps
- No primary data were retrieved for wine (~12%) or spirits (~40%); the often-quoted figure of roughly 10 ml extra urine per gram of ethanol (Eggleton 1942) is background knowledge I could not verify here, so no exact "break-even ABV" can be cited.
- All alcohol studies are small, in young men, and mostly post-exercise.

## 5. How much water do foods contribute (fruit, vegetables, soup, rice, phở/bún)? Any Vietnamese or Southeast Asian data?

### Takeaway
Food typically supplies about 20-22% of total water intake in US data, and more in cuisines rich in soups, rice and produce; official "adequate intake" figures (EFSA 2.0 L women / 2.5 L men) are total water including food, not drinking targets. No measured food-water data for Vietnamese diets were found.

### Cited Findings
- US: about 22% of total water intake comes from food; a separate US analysis gave 20.7%. The share "would be much higher in European countries", with Greece and South Korea named as high food-water diets (no percentage given). — [Popkin et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2908954/)
- Water content of foods (USDA data, Popkin Table 1): 90-99%: fat-free milk, watermelon, cantaloupe, strawberries, lettuce, cabbage, celery, spinach, cooked squash. 80-89%: fruit juice, yogurt, apples, grapes, oranges, carrots, cooked broccoli, pears, pineapple. 70-79%: bananas, avocados, baked potato, cooked corn, shrimp. 60-69%: pasta, legumes, salmon, chicken breast. 50-59%: ground beef. 30-39%: bread, cheddar. 1-9%: nuts, crackers, cereals. 0%: oils, sugars. — [Popkin et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2908954/)
- EFSA 2010 adequate intake of total water: 2.0 L/day for women and 2.5 L/day for men (age 14+); the value covers water from food plus beverages. — [EFSA press release 2010](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/press/news/nda100326); [secondary study noting scope](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5409722)
- US IOM adequate intake as quoted by Maughan et al.: 3.7 L for men and 3.0 L for women. **Flag**: the IOM 2004/2005 report value for women is, to my knowledge, 2.7 L; the 3.0 L in the manuscript looks like an error, so quote IOM from the original report. — [Maughan et al. 2016](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/12468)
- Participants in the BHI trial reported habitual water intake of 2.0 (0.8) L/day, a reminder of how widely normal intake varies. — [Maughan et al. 2016, Table 1](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/12468/1/Maughan%20et%20al%20AJCN%20Beverage%20hydration%20index%20final%20accepted%20manuscript%20with%20DOI%20NPW%20edits.pdf)

### Inferences
- A Vietnamese diet built on cooked rice, canh (soup) with most meals, phở/bún noodle soups, and fresh fruit and vegetables is plausibly in the "much higher than 22%" group, similar to the South Korean pattern Popkin mentions. That supports setting the app's drinking goal below the total-water adequate intake (e.g. a beverage goal near 1.5-2.0 L rather than 2.0-2.5 L), but this is an extrapolation.
- Logging soup broth as a drink is reasonable (it is mostly water, and sodium aids retention as with ORS); the caveat is salt load, not hydration. A bowl of phở can be offered as an optional "soup" entry with an estimated broth volume the user actually drinks.
- Do not ask users to log solid food water; instead bake the food contribution into the default goal.

### Gaps
- No source found with measured water content of cooked rice, phở, bún, canh, or cháo, nor any Vietnamese or Southeast Asian total-water-intake survey. The Vietnamese RDA book ("Nhu cầu dinh dưỡng khuyến nghị cho người Việt Nam", Viện Dinh dưỡng, 2016) reportedly contains water recommendations but was not accessed.
- EFSA's own statement of the food share (commonly quoted as 20-30%) could not be verified from the opinion text.
- Broth volume and sodium of typical noodle-soup servings: no data found.

## 6. Self-check markers: urine colour, urination frequency, thirst, body weight, skin turgor

### Takeaway
Urine colour is the best-studied lay marker and is moderately accurate at best when self-scored (AUC about 0.74-0.86; cut-offs shift with lighting and container), 24-h void count is a promising but thinly validated zero-equipment marker (about 5-7 voids when well hydrated versus 3-4 when under-hydrated), and no single sign is reliable enough to be presented as a diagnosis. Present them as a combined, low-stakes "check", not a measurement.

### Cited Findings

**Urine colour**
- The 8-colour chart was created by Armstrong et al. (1994); the sample is subjectively matched to one of eight colours. — [Wardenaar et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8069841/)
- Lay self-scoring validation, n = 189 athletes, coaches and ROTC cadets (99 male, 90 female), 8-colour Armstrong chart versus a newer 7-colour chart (Wardenaar & Bacalzo 2019), judged against USG < 1.020 and osmolality < 800 mmol/kg:
  - 8-colour vs USG: AUC 0.86, sensitivity 95.1%, specificity 56.3%, accuracy 77.2%, optimal cut-off colour <= 2.
  - 8-colour vs osmolality: AUC 0.76, sensitivity 47.5%, specificity 91.3%, accuracy 63.5%.
  - 7-colour vs USG: AUC 0.83, sensitivity 68.6%, specificity 86.2%, accuracy 76.7%.
  - 7-colour vs osmolality: AUC 0.74, sensitivity 57.5%, specificity 81.2%, accuracy 66.1%.
  - Charts agreed exactly in 60.5% of samples and within one shade in 96.5%; false positives 21% (8-colour) vs 6% (7-colour).
  — [Wardenaar et al. 2021, IJERPH](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8069841/)
- Same study on limits: bright LED light (~1650 lux) may shift cut-offs 1-2 shades lower than in ordinary rooms; container type and diameter change perceived colour; recent drinking dilutes urine; riboflavin (vitamin B2) is cited as a possible distorter though supplement users showed no substantial AUC difference here; trained technicians reached AUC 0.96 in earlier work. Authors' advice: use the chart as a sliding scale, not a fixed cut-off, and combine with other markers. — [Wardenaar et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8069841/)
- Systematic review (Kostelnik et al. 2020, J Am Coll Nutr): only 10 articles; studies generally reported high sensitivity of urine colour for detecting dehydration; validity when used by patients/clients has not been properly investigated **[abstract only]**. — [Kostelnik et al. 2020](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/07315724.2020.1750073)
- Field study in heat-exposed cyclists: colour score >= 5 identified body-mass loss >= 2% with 88.9% sensitivity and 84.8% specificity **[search summary; single study, exercise setting]**. — [PMC9253385](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9253385)
- Children aged 8-14: 8-point scale sensitivity 92-98%, specificity 55-68% for hypohydration **[search summary; not adults]**. — [Springer Medicine record](https://www.springermedicine.com/validation-of-a-urine-color-scale-for-assessment-of-urine-osmola/20939944)
- Older adults (Cochrane, Hooper et al. 2015, 67 tests reviewed): urine colour, urine volume, dry mouth, feeling thirsty and heart rate miss a high proportion of dehydrated people and wrongly label hydrated ones; no single test was consistently useful; individual tests should not be used alone in this population. — [Cochrane featured review](https://www.cochrane.org/about-us/news/featured-review-clinical-symptoms-signs-and-tests-identification-impending-and-current-water)

**Urination frequency**
- Burchfield et al. 2015 (Eur J Clin Nutr), 87 adults: euhydrated people voided 5 +/- 2 times per 24 h versus 3 +/- 1 in hypohydrated people (P < 0.001); void count correlated inversely with urine specific gravity and osmolality; proposed as a no-equipment self-assessment **[abstract only]**. — [PubMed 25604776](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25604776/)
- Reliability study 2016 (young males): 24-h void frequency is a reliable index when voiding at a consistent urgency; within-subject variation 15 +/- 9% euhydrated and 21 +/- 14% hypohydrated **[abstract only]**. — [PubMed 26862006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26862006/)
- Tucker et al. conference abstract (healthy males, four beverage mixes): void frequency 5.1 to 5.6 per 24 h, not different between beverage types; inversely related to 24-h USG and osmolality. — [IJESAB abstract](https://digitalcommons.wku.edu/ijesab/vol11/iss2/68)
- Undergraduate thesis (Ziebart 2017): daytime-only counts approximate 24-h counts; about 7 voids when hydrated versus about 4 when dehydrated **[thesis, weak]**. — [University of Arkansas thesis](https://scholarworks.uark.edu/hhpruht/49)

**Thirst**
- In healthy people in temperate climates most drinking is habitual and thirst "plays little day-to-day role"; thirst sensation declines in older adults. — [Popkin et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2908954/)
- For exercise, the 2015 international consensus (Hew-Butler et al.) states the safest individual strategy is to "drink palatable fluids when thirsty", sufficient to prevent both dehydration and hyponatremia. — [Wolters Kluwer release on the consensus statement](https://www.wolterskluwer.com/en/news/drink-when-thirsty-to-avoid-fatal-drops-in-blood-sodium-levels-during-exercise)
- In older people, "feeling thirsty" performed poorly as a stand-alone test. — [Cochrane featured review](https://www.cochrane.org/about-us/news/featured-review-clinical-symptoms-signs-and-tests-identification-impending-and-current-water)

**Body-weight change**
- Used as the reference index of euhydration in lab studies (near-nude body mass to the nearest 50 g, people on energy-restricted diets excluded because weight then stops being a hydration index). — [Maughan et al. 2016](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/12468)

**Skin turgor**
- Listed by the Cochrane review among simple tests commonly recommended for older people; the review found no single test consistently useful. I found no accuracy figure for skin turgor specifically. — [Cochrane featured review](https://www.cochrane.org/about-us/news/featured-review-clinical-symptoms-signs-and-tests-identification-impending-and-current-water)

### Inferences
- Evidence quality ranking for lay office users (my judgement): urine colour = moderate (several validation studies, but in athletes, children and elderly, not office workers); void frequency = low to moderate (one group, small samples, urine-concentration reference only); thirst = physiologically sound for healthy adults but not validated as a "status" test; day-to-day body weight = accurate in principle but impractical and confounded by food and bowel content for sedentary users; skin turgor = not supported for healthy adults.
- Is there a validated simplified chart for an app? Partly. The 7-colour Wardenaar chart is validated against USG/osmolality with similar accuracy to Armstrong's, and both charts' practical cut-off is "shade 1-2 = fine, 3 and darker = drink". No validated 3- or 4-colour chart was found, and no chart has been validated on a phone screen (emitted light, auto-brightness, night mode and colour profiles differ from printed charts). A 3-band display (pale / yellow / dark) is therefore a reasonable simplification but should be labelled as a rough guide.
- The high-sensitivity/low-specificity profile of the 8-colour chart (95% / 56% vs USG) means it over-calls dehydration: many users with fine hydration would be told to drink more. For a reminder app that is a tolerable error direction, but it argues against alarming wording.
- A workable in-app self-check: (1) "How many times have you urinated since waking?" with fewer than ~4 by evening as a prompt; (2) 3-band colour pick with caveats (B vitamins turn urine bright yellow, some foods and medicines colour it, first-morning urine is normally darker, look in daylight against a white bowl); (3) thirst as a third signal. Two of three pointing the same way is more trustworthy than any one, echoing the Cochrane and Wardenaar advice to combine markers. Armstrong's "WUT" (weight, urine, thirst) Venn approach is the published version of this idea, but I did not retrieve it in this session.

### Gaps
- Armstrong 1994/1998 original validation numbers were not retrieved.
- Effects of specific foods (beetroot, dragon fruit), drugs and vitamins on colour: only riboflavin was mentioned in a source; the rest is common clinical knowledge without a citation here.
- No validation of any marker in sedentary office workers, in Vietnamese populations, or via smartphone display.
- No source defining a "normal" daytime void count for adults with typical intake beyond the Arkansas group's work; urology norms (often quoted as up to ~7-8 per day) were not researched.
- Skin turgor accuracy in healthy adults: no data found.

## 7. Myths and what the evidence says

### Takeaway
Most popular hydration rules are unsupported rather than disproven: "8 glasses" has no experimental basis, extra water for skin/toxins/headache has little or mixed trial support, and the benefits with RCT backing are narrow (kidney-stone recurrence, recurrent UTI in women, modest weight loss with pre-meal water or when replacing caloric drinks). Honest app copy is "drink enough, not as much as possible".

### Cited Findings

**"8 glasses / 2 litres a day"**
- Valtin 2002 (Am J Physiol Regul Integr Comp Physiol 283:R993-R1004) searched for the origin and reported finding no published scientific evidence supporting "8 x 8"; he pointed to the 1945 US Food and Nutrition Board recommendation (about 1 ml of water per kcal of food), whose accompanying note that most of this is contained in prepared foods was overlooked **[secondary reports of the paper]**. — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/fact-or-fiction-you-must-drink-8-glasses-of-water-daily/); [The Dartmouth, 2002](https://www.thedartmouth.com/article/2002/08/professor-finds-8x8-water-consumption-is-not-so-great)
- Negoianu & Goldfarb 2008 editorial "Just add water" (JASN 19(6):1041-3): no clear evidence of benefit from drinking increased amounts of water, and also no clear evidence of lack of benefit; "there is simply a lack of evidence in general". — [PubMed 18385417](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18385417/); [press summary](https://www.brightsurf.com/news/L3YNZ701/evidence-lacking-on-health-benefits-of-drinking-lots-of-water.html)
- Official adequate intakes are for total water from all drinks and food (EFSA 2.0 / 2.5 L), not glasses of plain water. — [EFSA 2010](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/press/news/nda100326)
- 2024 systematic review of 18 RCTs: the trials "did not establish an optimal or target total daily water intake". — [Hakam et al. 2024, JAMA Netw Open](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11589796/)

**"More water is always better"**
- Drinking beyond need can cause hyponatremia; the 2015 consensus on exercise-associated hyponatremia recommends drinking to thirst and notes that over-drinking during exercise is the main cause, with free-water excretion impaired by antidiuretic hormone. — [Wolters Kluwer release](https://www.wolterskluwer.com/en/news/drink-when-thirsty-to-avoid-fatal-drops-in-blood-sodium-levels-during-exercise)
- In one RCT, about 30 ml/kg/day of extra water for 2 weeks raised daytime blood pressure in healthy adults; four other trials of extra water on kidney function, copeptin, blood pressure and quality of life were null. — [Hakam et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11589796/)
- In a trial for overactive bladder, reducing fluid intake by 25% improved symptoms, i.e. more is not better for everyone. — [Hakam et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11589796/)

**"Detox" / flushing toxins, and clear skin**
- Popkin et al. call drinking water to "flush toxins from the skin" a myth with "a general lack of evidence"; water intake can improve skin hydration, but "adequate skin hydration, however, is not sufficient to prevent wrinkles or other signs of aging". The kidneys "function more efficiently in the presence of an abundant water supply", but no study is cited showing that extra water removes more toxins in healthy people. — [Popkin et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2908954/)
- Akdeniz et al. 2018 systematic review (Skin Res Technol 24(3):459-465): additional water intake may slightly increase stratum corneum and "deep" skin hydration, mainly in people with low prior intake; effects on dryness, transepidermal water loss, sebum and pH unclear; included studies (5 or 6, sources differ) had methodological limitations **[abstract only]**. — [PubMed 29392767](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29392767/)

**Weight loss**
- Hakam et al. 2024: 4 RCTs on weight; 3 positive (Parretti, Akers, Dennis: about 500 ml before meals, or 1420 ml/day pre-meal, over 12 weeks to 12 months, in adults with overweight/obesity) and 1 null (Wong, adolescents, 1920 ml/day). Greater weight loss than control ranged from 44% to 100% more. — [Hakam et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11589796/)
- Contradicting pooled estimate: meta-analysis of 8 RCTs found a non-significant overall effect, WMD -0.33 kg (95% CI -1.75 to 1.08, I2 = 78%). — [Nutrients 2024, PubMed 38612997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38612997/)
- Bracamontes-Castelo et al. 2019 systematic review (6 RCTs): evidence quality low to moderate; replacing caloric beverages with water was the most effective strategy **[abstract only]**. — [PubMed 31657610](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31657610/)
- Negoianu & Goldfarb noted in 2008 that no carefully designed trials had measured water intake and weight maintenance (now partly superseded by the trials above). — [press summary](https://www.brightsurf.com/news/L3YNZ701/evidence-lacking-on-health-benefits-of-drinking-lots-of-water.html)

**Headaches**
- Water deprivation can trigger headache. In an RCT of 1.5 L/day extra water, the number of headache episodes was unaffected, with a modest association with lower intensity; "water is limited as prophylaxis in headache sufferers". — [Popkin et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2908954/)
- Two RCTs by Spigt (1500 ml/day extra, about 3 months): 2012 trial improved migraine-specific quality-of-life score but days with headache did not change significantly; 2005 pilot was null. — [Hakam et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11589796/)

**Benefits that do have RCT support (useful as "true" counterweights to the myths)**
- Kidney stones: 2 of 2 trials positive; Borghi et al. (urine output of at least 2000 ml/day for 5 years) about 15 fewer stone events per 100 participants. Recurrent UTI: Hooton et al., +1500 ml/day for 12 months reduced episodes in women with low baseline intake. Fasting glucose: 1 of 2 trials positive (type 2 diabetes patients). — [Hakam et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11589796/)
- Evidence quality in that review: 18 RCTs, median sample 48 (range 14-631); 50% low risk of bias, 27% some concerns, 22% high risk; no meta-analysis possible; authors call the evidence limited. — [Hakam et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11589796/)

**Warm vs cold water**
- Research on drink temperature and gastric motility is sparse: a 1995 human study notes "little information" on how temperature affects gastric emptying. Popular claims that cold water harms digestion trace to unverifiable or mutually contradictory secondary sources. — [PubMed 7590426](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7590426/)
- In the BHI trial, black tea served hot (60 C) and cold (4-6 C) gave indistinguishable urine output and fluid balance, so temperature did not change hydration value. — [Maughan et al. 2016](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/12468)

### Inferences
- "If you are thirsty you are already dehydrated": the cited material points the other way for healthy adults (thirst-guided drinking is considered sufficient even in endurance sport, and daily balance returns to baseline over 24 h). The caveats are older adults (blunted thirst) and people absorbed in work who ignore or do not notice thirst, which is exactly the niche a reminder app fills. Suggested framing: "thirst is a normal signal, not an emergency; reminders help when you are too busy to notice it".
- "More is always better": the app should have a soft upper guard (e.g. no praise for exceeding the goal by a large margin, and a gentle note if someone logs very large volumes in a short time), since sedentary users in air conditioning have low sweat losses.
- Weight-loss claims should be limited to "water before meals and instead of sweet drinks may modestly help"; the pooled effect is not statistically significant.
- Skin and detox claims should not be used as motivational copy; the supportable statement is "your kidneys do the detox; they just need enough water".
- Headache copy: "not drinking enough can trigger headaches in some people; extra water is not a proven treatment".

### Gaps
- Valtin 2002 and the 1945 Food and Nutrition Board text were not read directly; the origin story rests on secondary reporting. Valtin's specific argument on thirst (thirst begins at a plasma osmolality rise of roughly 2% while dehydration is usually defined from about 5%) is from my background knowledge and could not be verified here.
- Lemon water on waking: no peer-reviewed evidence found for or against any specific benefit beyond the water itself; treat as unsupported. (Acidic drinks and dental erosion is a plausible caveat I did not research.)
- Drinking during meals harming digestion: no clinical source retrieved (the Mayo Clinic expert answer could not be found via search). The pre-meal water trials above gave 500 ml before meals without reported digestive harm, which is indirect evidence only.
- Warm vs cold water: no good-quality comparative trial for digestion, metabolism or "health" in healthy adults was found; claims in both directions online were unverifiable.
- Safe upper intake rate: the commonly quoted renal maximum of roughly 0.7-1.0 L/hour could not be verified from a primary source.
- Constipation, kidney function (CKD) and cardiovascular claims were not researched.

## 8. Does mild dehydration (1-2% body mass) impair cognition, mood or work performance in office settings?

### Takeaway
The pooled evidence shows a small cognitive impairment that is clear only above 2% body-mass loss; at or below 2% the effect is about half the size and its confidence interval touches zero. Mood, fatigue and perceived effort are affected more consistently than objective performance, and almost no studies were done in real offices.

### Cited Findings
- Wittbrodt & Millard-Stafford 2018 meta-analysis (Med Sci Sports Exerc 50(11)): 33 studies, 280 effect sizes, 413 subjects, dehydration of 1-6% body-mass loss. Overall impairment small but significant: ES = -0.21 (95% CI -0.31 to -0.11, P < 0.0001). Studies with > 2% loss: ES = -0.28 (95% CI -0.41 to -0.16); <= 2%: ES = -0.14 (95% CI -0.27 to 0.00); difference P = 0.04. Most affected: attention, executive function, motor coordination; simple reaction time least affected. Significant heterogeneity **[abstract via search plus university press release]**. — [Georgia Tech release](https://news.gatech.edu/news/2018/07/16/we-get-parched-cognition-can-sputter-dehydration-study-says); [abstract record](https://vufind-test.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c4222-9b406a0bb40ab5a1b1408fb544b38960f90cf5c6c9617a58e36211d701d31a072)
- Popkin et al. 2010: mild dehydration "does not appear to alter cognitive functioning in a consistent manner"; in some studies performance was not significantly affected at 2-2.6% dehydration; in one study "the only consistent effect of mild dehydration was significant elevations of subjective mood score" (fatigue, confusion, anger). — [Popkin et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2908954/)
- Work-relevant single studies cited by Maughan et al. (not read here): mild hypohydration increased driver errors during a prolonged monotonous driving task (Watson et al. 2015, Physiol Behav); hydration among on-call nurses and doctors (El-Sharkawy et al. 2015, the HANDS study); hydration status and beverage intake in the workplace (Mears & Shirreffs 2014). Note these come from the same EHI-linked research network. — [Maughan et al. 2016 reference list](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/12468/1/Maughan%20et%20al%20AJCN%20Beverage%20hydration%20index%20final%20accepted%20manuscript%20with%20DOI%20NPW%20edits.pdf)
- Under normal free-living conditions "large fluid deficits are uncommon for the majority of the population" because body water balance returns to the same point over a 24-h cycle. — [Maughan et al. 2016](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/12468)

### Inferences
- Reaching 1-2% body-mass loss means losing 0.6-1.2 kg of water for a 60 kg person without replacing it. That is typical of exercise or heat exposure, not of sitting in an air-conditioned office with access to drinks. Most lab studies induce dehydration by exercise, heat or 12-24 h fluid restriction, so heat stress and fatigue are confounded with dehydration and the results transfer poorly to office workers.
- The honest claim for app copy: "going long stretches without drinking may leave you feeling more tired and less focused" (mood/fatigue evidence), not "drinking water boosts productivity/IQ". Avoid numeric claims such as "2% dehydration cuts performance by X%".
- The Vietnamese context adds a real but unquantified factor: commuting by motorbike in heat and humidity, then long hours in dry air-conditioned rooms where thirst cues are weak. That makes morning-arrival and post-lunch reminders plausible, but no data I found measure hydration status in Vietnamese office workers.
- Effect sizes of -0.14 to -0.28 are small; individual users are unlikely to notice a performance change, so self-reported "I feel better" should not be over-interpreted (placebo/expectation effects are likely).

### Gaps
- No study of hydration and cognition or productivity in office workers in Vietnam or Southeast Asia was found.
- I did not retrieve water-supplementation trials in already-euhydrated adults (does drinking extra improve cognition?) or the mood studies by Armstrong 2012 / Ganio 2011 / Pross; the writer should treat "extra water improves focus in normally hydrated adults" as unverified.
- Much of this literature is funded by beverage or hydration-industry bodies (EHI, Danone/Hydration for Health, sports-drink makers); funding of the Wittbrodt meta-analysis itself was not checked.
- Full text of Wittbrodt 2018 was not read; heterogeneity statistics and risk-of-bias assessment are unknown.
