# Timing, pacing and safety limits of drinking water, and who should not follow a generic target (as of Nov 2026)

Scope note: population of interest is healthy, mostly sedentary working-age adults in air-conditioned offices in a hot, humid country. Research was done by web search on 2026-11-18. Where a finding comes from a search-result summary or a secondary source rather than the primary document read in full, it is marked "(secondary)". Numbers that I recall but did not retrieve in this session are listed under Gaps, not under Cited Findings.

## 1. Is evenly spaced sipping better than large boluses?

### Takeaway
Small trials suggest that spreading the same volume over time loses less of it to urine than drinking it in one go (about 75% vs 55% retained in the best-known study), but the evidence is thin: 8-9 young men per study, post-exercise rehydration, and one volume-matched trial found no difference. It supports the app's even-spacing design as reasonable, not as proven.

### Cited Findings
- Jones et al. 2010: eight men drank water as a rapid bolus or on a slower metered schedule after exercise. Mean urine output 700 ml (bolus) vs 420 ml (metered); hydration efficiency 55% (bolus) vs 75% (metered), p = .018. Authors concluded metered intake maintains fluid balance better. (secondary: search summary of the PubMed record) — [PubMed 20479487](https://www.pubmed.gov/20479487/); [abstract mirror](https://coachsci.sdsu.edu/csa/vol162/jones.htm)
- 2020 study with a carbohydrate-protein beverage over about 6 hours: urine production 1167 ± 293 ml (bolus) vs 730 ± 324 ml (metered), p = 0.003; authors concluded a metered pattern improves fluid retention. (secondary) — [Int J Exerc Sci 13(2)](https://digitalcommons.wku.edu/ijes/vol13/iss2/26)
- Null result: nine men dehydrated by about 2% body weight and given 150% fluid replacement showed no difference between methods: hydration efficiency 41.5% (bolus) vs 42% (metered), p = .94; urine 1347 ml vs 1337 ml. (secondary) — [Graham et al., IJES conference abstract](https://digitalcommons.wku.edu/ijesab/vol2/iss2/27)
- 2024 preprint found lower urine output with a metered commercial beverage than with bolus water, but the authors say the difference could be due to ingestion rate and/or beverage composition, so it does not isolate timing. (secondary) — [medRxiv 2024.09.28.24314547](https://medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.28.24314547v1.full.pdf+html)
- Drinking above thirst is hard to sustain: an overhydration review states it requires "a threefold increase in volitional effort" and that most free-living adults cannot sustain even 500 mL/day above thirst. — [Hew-Butler et al., "Of Mice and Men—The Physiology, Psychology, and Pathology of Overhydration", PMC6682940](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6682940/)

### Inferences
- The direction of effect is consistent (bolus → more urine) when total volume roughly equals the deficit, and disappears when the volume is far above need (150% replacement). For a sedentary office worker who is not dehydrated, the practical gain from metering is probably small; the main benefits of even spacing are comfort, fewer urgent toilet trips, and staying far below hourly safety limits.
- A 350 ml default cup is a moderate bolus; a 100 ml sip is closer to "metered". Nothing in the evidence found says 350 ml at once is harmful or wasteful for a healthy adult. The app should not claim that sipping "hydrates better" as fact; "spreading intake through the day is a sensible habit" is defensible.

### Gaps
- All studies found are in exercise-dehydrated young men. I found no trial of bolus vs metered drinking in euhydrated sedentary adults, in women, or in older adults.
- I did not read the Jones 2010 full text, so the exact volumes and schedule (bolus duration, metered interval) are not confirmed here.

## 2. Maximum renal water excretion, hourly/daily limits, water intoxication, exercise-associated hyponatremia

### Takeaway
A healthy resting kidney clears roughly 0.7-1.0 L of water per hour, so sustained intake above about 1 L/hour is the zone where dilutional hyponatremia becomes possible; documented poisonings in healthy people typically involve 3-4 L within about an hour or several litres over a few hours. The best review available says no safe threshold can be firmly stated, because excretion capacity falls sharply when vasopressin is raised (exercise, illness, certain drugs).

### Cited Findings
Excretion capacity
- A physiologist quoted by Scientific American: a resting kidney excretes 800 to 1,000 mL/hour; during a marathon, vasopressin can cut that to as low as 100 mL/hour. (journalism quoting an expert, not a primary measurement) — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/strange-but-true-drinking-too-much-water-can-kill/)
- An oral fluid-overload study reported free water clearance peaking at 11.2 mL/min after 2 hours. (secondary: search summary) — [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/478309123.pdf)
- Urine excretion of about 1 L/h is seen in diabetes insipidus and compulsive water drinkers (vasopressin suppressed); "maximal urine excretion rates allow humans to tolerate water intakes approximating 20 L per day", but "the actual fluid intake tolerance limit appears to be closer to 10 L per day in normal individuals." — [Hew-Butler et al., PMC6682940](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6682940/)
- The same review says no safe threshold can be firmly recommended: both about 3 L/h and about 10 L/day are tolerated by some people and fatal to others. — [PMC6682940](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6682940/)

Intake thresholds linked to harm
- Healthy people at rest: acute intake of "around 3–4 L (~1 gallon)" within about 1 hour can cause symptomatic hyponatremia. With non-osmotic vasopressin secretion or severe sodium loss, "modest water ingestion at rates of 1–2 L/h can induce symptomatic hyponatremia." Review conclusion: complications are uncommon at rest but may occur above 3 L/h acutely or 5–10+ L/day chronically. — [PMC6682940](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6682940/)
- In primary polydipsia, intake that exceeds 400 to 600 mL/hour is described as potentially fatal (context: psychiatric patients, often with impaired excretion). (secondary) — [UCLA DOM Proceedings](https://journalpub.escholarship.org/ucladom_proceedings/article/45046/galley/33839/download)

Cases (all case reports; no pooled lethal dose exists)
- 6 L of water over 3.5 hours → water intoxication with hypo-osmolar hyponatremia (non-fatal). — [PMC8484258](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8484258/)
- About 8 L over three hours before a bladder-filling test, and 5-6 L in a short window while waiting to give a urine sample (non-fatal cases). (secondary: search summary of case reports) — [PMC3924712](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3924712/); [PMC13131036](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13131036/)
- 4 L of clear fluid within two hours for bowel preparation after low solute intake → acute water intoxication; authors link it to impaired free-water clearance from low dietary solute. — [PMC5297160](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5297160/)
- Historical/table cases in the review: 3 L in 20 minutes (Rowntree 1923, experimental); 10 L/day as 250–500 mL every 30–60 min for 11 days produced headache, cognitive and mood symptoms but no death (De Wardener and Herxheimer); 6 L in 2 h plus a 1 L enema for constipation (Swanson 1958); fatal cases after "plenty of water" taken for gastroenteritis or to dilute a poison; first recorded fatality in 1935 after 9 L over 24 h given rectally. — [PMC6682940](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6682940/)
- Fatal water intoxication in a woman who compulsively drank an estimated 30 to 40 glasses. — [Fatal water intoxication, PMC1770067](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1770067/?report=classic)
- Among compulsive water drinkers, hyponatremia (sodium < 135 mmol/L) is reported in 10% to 20%. (secondary) — [PMC10191386](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10191386/)

Exercise-associated hyponatremia (EAH)
- Third International EAH Consensus Development Conference statement (Hew-Butler et al., Clin J Sport Med 2015, DOI 10.1097/JSM.0000000000000221): drinking too much is the main cause of EAH; drinking palatable fluid when thirsty is described as the safest individualised strategy; it warns against "forced hydration" and against advice to "drink before you get thirsty". It was convened after two high-school football players died of dilutional EAH. (secondary: press releases; full statement not read) — [Wolters Kluwer release](https://www.wolterskluwer.com/en/news/drink-when-thirsty-to-avoid-fatal-drops-in-blood-sodium-levels-during-exercise); [Loyola release](https://ssom.luc.edu/news/stories/waterstory.html); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/809627)
- Funding note: the conference was supported by CrossFit, Inc.; reports say no CrossFit members took part in writing the guidelines. — [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/809627)
- Some marathon runners drank 80–100 cups of fluid during a race. — [PMC6682940](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6682940/)

### Inferences
- 11.2 mL/min peak free water clearance equals about 670 mL/hour, which matches the low end of the commonly quoted 0.7-1.0 L/hour. (my arithmetic)
- Design limits that follow, with a wide margin below every documented harm threshold:
  - Soft hourly cap around 700-800 ml logged in any rolling 60 minutes (two default 350 ml cups); show a gentle "slow down" message rather than blocking.
  - Firm warning at or above about 1 L in an hour, and again if this repeats in consecutive hours (cases cluster at ≥ 1.5-2 L/hour sustained for 2-3 hours, or 3-4 L in one hour).
  - Daily sanity warning somewhere around 3.5-4 L for a sedentary office user, well under the 5-10 L/day range where the review places chronic risk. The exact number is a product decision, not a physiological constant.
- The 1 L/hour figure is not safe for everyone: it assumes normal kidneys, normal vasopressin and normal solute intake. People on the drugs in section 5, people who are ill (vomiting, diarrhoea), fasting or eating very little, and people exercising hard can get into trouble at 1-2 L/hour or less.
- "Catch-up" behaviour is the realistic risk in a reminder app: a user who missed reminders drinks several cups at once to hit the daily goal. The app should never suggest making up a deficit in one go, and should let a missed target lapse.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm a primary, peer-reviewed measurement that states "maximum 0.7-1.0 L/hour" in those words; the range is widely repeated on consumer sites and attributed (unverified) to the National Academies. Treat it as an approximate consensus figure.
- The EAH consensus statement itself was not read; exact wording and its numeric examples are unverified. From memory (not verified here): the statement defines EAH as serum sodium < 135 mmol/L during or up to 24 h after activity, and the Boston Marathon study (Almond et al., NEJM 2005) found about 13% of sampled finishers hyponatremic.
- No systematic review giving a pooled "toxic dose" of water was found; everything above is case-level.

## 3. Evidence on specific timings (waking, before meals, with meals, evening)

### Takeaway
Only two timings have real evidence: about 500 ml roughly 30 minutes before main meals modestly helps weight loss in adults with overweight or obesity (about 1-2 kg extra over 12 weeks, small trials), and cutting fluids in the last 2-4 hours before bed is standard first-line advice for nocturia, though supported by expert opinion more than trials. Water with meals does not harm digestion. I found no good evidence for a special benefit of drinking on waking.

### Cited Findings
Before meals
- Dennis et al. 2010 (Obesity): 48 adults aged 55–75, BMI 25–40, randomised to a hypocaloric diet plus 500 ml water before each daily meal, or the diet alone, for 12 weeks. The water group lost about 2 kg more (a decline about 44% larger); a thesis on the same trial reports 7.4 kg vs 5.5 kg. — [PubMed 19661958](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19661958/); [Virginia Tech thesis](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/handle/10919/37616)
- Parretti et al. 2015 (Obesity): 84 adults with obesity from UK general practices; 500 ml water 30 min before main meals (n = 41) vs an attention control asked to imagine a full stomach (n = 43). At 12 weeks the water group lost 1.3 kg more (95% CI −2.4 to −0.1, p = 0.028); after adjustment for ethnicity, deprivation, age and gender the difference was −1.2 kg with p = 0.063. Authors call it "preliminary evidence". — [University of Birmingham record](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/efficacy-of-water-preloading-before-main-meals-as-a-strategy-for-)
- The widely reported "4.3 kg vs 0.8 kg" figure in Parretti compares participants who said they preloaded before all three meals with those who did so once or never; it is a self-selected adherence comparison, not randomised. — [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/08/150826101645.htm)

With meals
- Mayo Clinic: water during or after a meal "doesn't cause problems with digestion or thin body fluids used in digestion"; it can help a person feel full without added calories. It adds that people with heart, kidney or liver disease may need to limit how much they drink. — [Mayo Clinic expert answer](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/nutrition-and-healthy-eating/expert-answers/digestion/faq-20058348)

Evening and nocturia
- EAU patient information (reviewed by guideline panel members): "Try to drink less in the evening, especially 2 hours before you go to sleep, to avoid waking up at night to pee." — [EAU Patient Information](https://patients.uroweb.org/condition/nocturia/treatment-options-nocturia/self-management-of-nocturia-symptoms)
- AUA/Urology Care Foundation fact sheet: "Drink plenty of fluids during the day but limit fluids 2-4 hours before going to bed"; diuretics should be taken 6 or more hours before sleep. — [AUA Nocturia Fact Sheet](https://webservices.auanet.org/DCDFiles/15886730/Nocturia%20Fact%20Sheet.pdf)
- EAU male LUTS guideline summary: conservative measures including limiting night-time fluid intake, reducing caffeine and alcohol and improving sleep hygiene can reduce nocturia episodes; the cheat sheet appears to show evidence level 4 (expert opinion). (secondary; table hard to parse) — [EAU Cheat Sheet](https://d56bochluxqnz.cloudfront.net/documents/EAU-Cheat-Sheet-EAU-Guidelines-on-Male-LUTS-V-Management-of-Nocturia.pdf)
- ICS 2019 consensus on nocturia: states only that lifestyle interventions "may be useful". (secondary) — [ICS consensus 2019](https://www.ics.org/folder/news-and-publications/d/international-continence-society-consensus-on-the-diagnosis-and-treatment-of-nocturia-2019/download)
- An ICS workshop handout concedes that decreasing fluid intake is advised "with limited evidence" and describes reducing evening fluid as common sense. (workshop material, not peer reviewed) — [ICS workshop handout](https://www.ics.org/Workshops/HandoutFiles/001004.pdf)
- Patient material also warns not to restrict total daily fluid unless a clinician says so. — [Grand Rounds in Urology handout](https://grandroundsinurology.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/Living-with-Nocturia.pdf)
- ICS definition of nocturia: waking at night one or more times to void, each void preceded and followed by sleep. (secondary) — [ICS consensus 2019](https://www.ics.org/folder/news-and-publications/d/international-continence-society-consensus-on-the-diagnosis-and-treatment-of-nocturia-2019/download)

### Inferences
- Reminder schedule: stop or strongly thin reminders 2 hours before the user's set bedtime as a default, with an optional 3-4 hour taper for users who say night-time urination bothers them. Shift the volume earlier in the day rather than lowering the daily total.
- A "before lunch" reminder about 30 minutes ahead of the meal is evidence-compatible, but the app must not present it as a weight-loss feature: the trials are in people with overweight/obesity on or near a diet, effects are small, and a weight-loss claim moves the app toward health claims that need substantiation (section 6).
- The 500 ml pre-meal volume used in the trials is larger than the app's 350 ml default cup; no need to match it.
- The app can safely tell users that water with meals is fine; this counters a common myth without making a medical claim.

### Gaps
- Drinking on waking: no trial or guideline was found in this session supporting a specific benefit of water immediately after waking (metabolism, "detox", bowel function). Claims of this kind circulate widely in consumer media; the report writer should treat them as unsupported unless another researcher found evidence. Physiologically an overnight fast without fluid leaves urine more concentrated in the morning, so a morning drink is reasonable, but that is reasoning, not trial evidence.
- I did not retrieve the recommendation text and strength rating from the current EAU non-neurogenic male LUTS guideline or the AUA/SUFU guideline; the 2-hour and 2-4-hour windows come from their patient-facing materials.
- No trial was found that tests a specific evening cut-off time against another.

## 4. Is thirst reliable in healthy adults, and how does it change with age?

### Takeaway
For healthy working-age adults, thirst is generally considered an adequate guide and "you are already dehydrated when thirsty" is not supported; reminders are therefore a convenience and habit aid, not a medical necessity. In adults over about 65, thirst after fluid loss is blunted (shown in small but classic experiments), so scheduled drinking is more justified for older users, who are also the group most likely to have conditions or drugs that make extra water risky.

### Cited Findings
- Valtin 2002 review of the "8 × 8" rule: "No scientific studies were found in support of 8 × 8"; conclusion limited to healthy adults in a temperate climate leading a largely sedentary existence. The review also disputes the claim that people are already dehydrated by the time they feel thirsty. — [Valtin 2002 (Dartmouth PDF)](https://geiselmed.dartmouth.edu/news/2002_h2/pdf/8x8.pdf); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/fact-or-fiction-you-must-drink-8-glasses-of-water-daily/)
- A panel conclusion cited in coverage: the vast majority of healthy people meet daily hydration needs by letting thirst be their guide. (secondary) — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/fact-or-fiction-you-must-drink-8-glasses-of-water-daily/)
- The overhydration review calls the one-size-fits-all eight-glasses guideline based on "equivocal evidence" and warns against "overzealous (but well-intended) advice to 'stay hydrated'". — [PMC6682940](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6682940/)
- A 2020 nutrition review notes thirst is implicitly assumed to be an adequate guide, while suggesting that drinking somewhat beyond thirst may help in specific cases such as kidney stones (a hypothesis in that paper, not an established general recommendation). (secondary) — [Eur J Nutr 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00394-020-02296-z)
- Phillips et al. 1984 (NEJM): after 24 hours of water deprivation, seven healthy active men aged 67–75 were less thirsty and drank less than seven men aged 20–31, and did not restore plasma and urine values to baseline, despite larger rises in plasma osmolality, sodium and vasopressin. — [PubMed 6472364](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6472364/)
- A 1991 follow-up found the reduced thirst in older men was mainly due to lower thirst sensitivity to hypertonicity, with a tendency toward a higher thirst threshold. (secondary) — [Reduced osmotic thirst in healthy elderly men](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/1009679499)
- A review notes that age-related changes in total body water, thirst perception, renal concentrating ability and vasopressin effectiveness probably predispose older people to dehydration. (secondary) — [PMC2625510](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2625510)

### Inferences
- Valtin's conclusion is explicitly limited to temperate climates. Vietnamese office workers sit in air-conditioning most of the day but commute and eat lunch in heat and humidity; sweat losses on those segments are not sensed in advance. Reminders around commute and lunch are easier to justify than a blanket claim that thirst is unreliable.
- The app should never say "by the time you are thirsty you are already dehydrated".
- If the app collects age, users ≥ 60-65 are a group where reminders have more physiological justification and also where the medical caveat in section 5 matters most (heart failure, CKD, thiazides and SSRIs are all far more common). The onboarding caveat should be shown to everyone but is most relevant to them.

### Gaps
- The thirst-ageing experiments are very small and male-only (n = 7 per group); I did not find larger or female replications in this session.
- No source was retrieved giving a numeric thirst threshold (the often-quoted "thirst begins at 1-2% body-mass loss") or the EFSA adequate-intake figures; another researcher's notes on daily targets may cover these.
- No data specific to Vietnamese or Southeast Asian office workers was found.

## 5. Conditions and medications for which a generic "drink more" target is unsafe

### Takeaway
People with heart failure, advanced kidney disease or dialysis, cirrhosis with ascites or low sodium, SIADH, or psychogenic polydipsia, and people taking thiazide diuretics, SSRIs/SNRIs, carbamazepine or desmopressin, should set their fluid target with their clinician rather than accept an app default. Guideline support for strict fluid restriction is weaker than commonly assumed (notably in stable heart failure after the 2025 FRESH-UP trial), but that does not make a push-to-drink-more app appropriate for these users.

### Cited Findings
Heart failure
- Commentary on FRESH-UP notes US and EU recommendations to limit fluid to about 1.5 L/day in this population, and that 2021 and 2022 guidelines themselves described the evidence as low quality. (secondary; guideline text not read) — [ACC press release](https://www.acc.org/about-acc/press-releases/2025/03/30/12/24/limiting-fluid-intake-may-not-be-needed-for-some-people-with-heart-failure)
- FRESH-UP (Herrmann et al., Nature Medicine 2025; presented ACC.25): open-label RCT, 504 outpatients with chronic heart failure NYHA II–III, liberal intake vs a cap of 1,500 ml/day. KCCQ overall summary score at 3 months 74.0 vs 72.2 (adjusted difference 2.17, not significant); thirst distress higher with restriction; no difference in death, HF hospitalisation, IV diuretic need or acute kidney injury at 6 months. Actual intake differed modestly: 1,480 mL/day vs 1,760 mL/day. Investigators concluded stable heart failure patients do not need fluid restriction. — [ACC press release](https://www.acc.org/about-acc/press-releases/2025/03/30/12/24/limiting-fluid-intake-may-not-be-needed-for-some-people-with-heart-failure); [PACE-CME summary](https://pace-cme.org/news/2473896/)

Liver cirrhosis
- EASL 2010 ascites guideline: fluid intake should be restricted only in patients with dilutional hyponatremia. Secondary summaries give 1 L/day restriction as first-line for hypervolemic hyponatremia, with sodium thresholds varying by source (< 125 mmol/L in most; < 120 for stopping diuretics in one). Fluid restriction has relatively low efficacy but remains standard of care in EASL and AASLD guidance. (secondary; current EASL text not read) — [Vanderbilt hepatology teaching page](https://vimbook.vumc.org/hepatology/hyponatremia-cirrhosis); [guideline comparison table, PMC7805288](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7805288/table/j_jtim-2020-0035_tab_002)

SIADH / hyponatraemia
- European clinical practice guideline on hyponatraemia (Spasovski et al. 2014; ERA-EDTA, ESE, ESICM): fluid restriction is first-line treatment for moderate or profound hyponatraemia in SIAD, followed by increased solute intake (urea or salt). (secondary) — [HCPLive summary](https://hcplive.com/view/new-european-hyponatremia-guidelines-released)
- A later review notes fluid restriction fails in roughly half of SIAD cases and its evidence base is limited. (secondary) — [Endocrine Reviews abstract via Scribd](https://www.scribd.com/document/663650956/Endocrinology-SIAD)

Psychogenic (primary) polydipsia
- Occurs often with schizophrenia, depression or anxiety; compulsive drinkers show abnormal thirst regulation; hyponatremia in 10-20% of compulsive water drinkers; fatal cases documented. — [PMC6682940](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6682940/); [PMC10191386](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10191386/); [PMC11955096](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11955096/)

General statement
- Mayo Clinic: people with heart, kidney or liver disease may need to limit the amount of water they drink. — [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/nutrition-and-healthy-eating/expert-answers/digestion/faq-20058348)
- The overhydration review lists kidney disease and fluid overload in elderly or critically ill patients among risk settings. — [PMC6682940](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6682940/)

Medications
- Thiazide diuretics: leading drug cause of hyponatremia; implicated in about half of drug-induced cases in one emergency-department cohort, hydrochlorothiazide most often. Thiazide-induced hyponatremia is the most common drug cause requiring hospital admission in the UK, affects a minority of users, and mostly appears in the first weeks to months. Predicting who is at risk beforehand is not currently possible (2024 preprint). — [PMC13010667](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13010667/); [PMC5893690](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5893690/); [medRxiv 2024](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.10.06.24314957.full.pdf)
- Antidepressants: 2024 meta-analysis event rates for hyponatremia: SNRIs 7.44%, SSRIs 5.59%, TCAs 2.66%, mirtazapine 1.02%, trazodone 0.89%. A 2014 review reported SSRI rates ranging from 0.06% to 40% across studies, with older age and concomitant thiazide use (odds ratios about 11 to 13.5) as risk factors. The two reviews disagree on whether SSRIs or SNRIs rank highest. — [PMC11860797](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11860797/); [PubMed 25262043](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25262043/)
- Carbamazepine: the mood stabiliser most commonly associated with hyponatremia; higher risk of hospitalisation for hyponatremia than valproic acid. — [PMC10911096](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10911096/)
- Other drugs linked to hyponatremia through non-osmotic vasopressin secretion: MDMA (Ecstasy), methamphetamines, first- and second-generation antipsychotics, antidepressants. — [PMC6682940](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6682940/)
- Desmopressin: appeared as a cause in one case (0.6%) in the emergency-department cohort; no dedicated review was retrieved. — [PMC13010667](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13010667/)

### Inferences
- A practical exclusion list for the onboarding screen ("check with your doctor before using a daily target if any of these apply"): heart failure; kidney disease, especially dialysis; liver cirrhosis; a history of low blood sodium; taking "water pills" (diuretics), antidepressants, anti-seizure medicine such as carbamazepine, or desmopressin; having been told by a doctor to limit fluids; pregnancy and breastfeeding can be listed as "needs differ" rather than as a risk.
- FRESH-UP weakens the case for strict restriction in stable heart failure but does not support encouraging such patients to drink more: both arms drank under 1.8 L/day, and the trial excluded unstable patients. The correct app behaviour is to defer to the clinician's number and let the user set a custom, lower target without nagging, streak penalties or "you are behind" messages.
- The app should allow a user-defined daily maximum (not only a goal) so that fluid-restricted users can use it as a limiter. That is a design idea; it must not be marketed as managing a disease (section 6).
- The drug risk is strongest in older adults and with combinations (thiazide + SSRI), which fits the age point in section 4.

### Gaps
- Guideline wording not verified in this session, recalled from memory only: ESC 2021 heart failure guideline (fluid restriction of 1.5–2 L/day "may be considered" in severe heart failure/hyponatraemia to relieve symptoms and congestion); 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA guideline (benefit of fluid restriction in advanced heart failure with hyponatremia "uncertain", weak recommendation). The report writer should confirm before quoting.
- KDIGO 2024 CKD guideline: no statement on fluid intake was retrieved. From memory, not verified: early CKD generally needs no fluid restriction, whereas dialysis patients with little or no urine output are commonly limited to roughly urine output plus 500–1000 ml/day to control weight gain between sessions.
- Desmopressin: its product labelling is known to carry a hyponatremia warning with advice to limit fluid intake, but I did not retrieve a citable source for that here.
- Other groups not researched: kidney stone formers (who are told to drink more), pregnancy, adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism, NSAID use, and low-solute diets ("tea and toast", beer potomania) beyond the single bowel-prep case report.

## 6. Disclaimers, regulatory positioning and claims to avoid

### Takeaway
A plain water-reminder app sits comfortably in the "general wellness / not a medical device" category in the US and on Google Play, provided it makes no claim to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any condition and does not tailor advice to a disease. Google Play concretely requires the Health apps declaration, a privacy policy, a not-a-medical-device disclaimer in the store description, and a reminder to consult a healthcare professional.

### Cited Findings
Google Play (applies directly to this Android app)

- FDA issued a revised final "General Wellness: Policy for Low Risk Devices" on 6 January 2026, replacing the 2019 version; the two-factor test is unchanged: intended only for general wellness use, and low risk. — [King & Spalding](https://www.kslaw.com/news-and-insights/fda-updates-general-wellness-and-clinical-decision-support-guidance-documents); [Troutman Pepper Locke](https://www.troutman.com/insights/fdas-2026-guidance-on-general-wellness-devices-policy-for-low-risk-devices/)

### Inferences
- Claims the app must avoid (store listing, in-app copy, AI-generated chat lines, notifications):
  - Disease claims: prevents or treats kidney stones, urinary infections, constipation, headaches/migraine, hypertension, diabetes, gout; "detoxes" or "flushes toxins".
  - Diagnostic language: "you are dehydrated", "your hydration level is low/abnormal", any hydration "score" presented as a physiological measurement. The app only knows what the user logged.
  - Outcome guarantees: weight loss, clearer skin, better kidney function, improved concentration presented as proven effects.
  - Individualised medical advice: a target adjusted for a disease or a drug, or telling a user with a condition how much to drink.
  - Statements against consensus, such as "everyone needs 8 glasses/2 L", "you are already dehydrated when thirsty", "more water is always better".
- Safe framing: "helps you build a regular water-drinking habit", "reminders you can adjust", "the daily goal is a general guide, not medical advice".
- Because this app generates text with an LLM (the repo has a Gemini client and offline lines), the claim rules must be enforced in the prompt and in the offline line set, not only in the store listing; a generated line such as "drink up to protect your kidneys" is a health claim made by the app.
- Suggested disclaimer (English source text; needs Vietnamese localisation and legal review): "HydraChat is a habit reminder, not a medical device. It does not diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any medical condition. The daily goal is a general guide for healthy adults. If you have a heart, kidney or liver condition, take diuretics or other regular medicines, are pregnant, or have been told to limit fluids, ask your doctor how much to drink and set your own goal. Do not drink large amounts in a short time to catch up."
- Placement: store description (required by Google Play), onboarding before the first goal is set, and a permanent entry in Settings.

## 7. Warning signs of overhydration and dehydration suitable for lay users

### Takeaway
Dehydration signs are well standardised by the NHS (thirst, dark strong-smelling urine, urinating less, headache, dizziness, tiredness). Early overhydration signs are nonspecific (headache, nausea, confusion) and overlap with dehydration, so the useful message for users is contextual: these symptoms after drinking a lot in a short time mean stop drinking and seek medical help, not drink more.

### Cited Findings
Dehydration
- NHS: symptoms in adults include feeling thirsty, dark yellow and strong-smelling pee, peeing less often than usual, feeling dizzy or lightheaded, and headache. — [NHS: Dehydration](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/dehydration/symptoms)
- Pale or clear urine is described as a good sign of being well hydrated. — [NI Direct](https://nidirect.gov.uk/node/10275)
- Urine colour is only part of the picture: some vitamins, medicines and foods change it. (secondary) — [Information Now (UK)](https://www.informationnow.org.uk/article/dehydration/)

Overhydration / water intoxication
- Symptoms recorded in volunteers drinking 10 L/day: headache, visual disturbance (scotoma), cold pale skin, facial puffiness, dry lips without thirst, food tasting bland, emotional lability and difficulty with simple tasks. — [PMC6682940](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6682940/)
- Severe hyponatremia causes brain swelling (encephalopathy) that can progress to brainstem herniation; the animal literature lists restlessness, lethargy, polyuria, nausea, vomiting, muscle twitching, seizures, coma and death. — [PMC6682940](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6682940/)
- Consumer-level summary: early symptoms may include headaches, confusion and nausea; severe confusion, marked drowsiness, seizures, loss of consciousness and trouble breathing need urgent care; early signs can be mistaken for dehydration, fatigue, heat illness or stomach upset. (hospital and commercial health sites, lower quality) — [Apollo Hospitals](https://www.apollohospitals.com/diseases-and-conditions/water-intoxication); [Ubie Health](https://ubiehealth.com/doctors-note/signs-drinking-too-much-water-overhydration-risk4793q6)
- EAH consensus: treatment is guided by symptom severity rather than by the sodium number alone. (secondary) — [Wolters Kluwer release](https://www.wolterskluwer.com/en/news/drink-when-thirsty-to-avoid-fatal-drops-in-blood-sodium-levels-during-exercise)

### Inferences
- Lay wording for "you may need more fluid": thirst, dark yellow strong-smelling urine, passing urine less than usual, headache, dizziness, tiredness, dry mouth. Aim for pale yellow urine; completely clear urine all day together with very frequent toilet trips suggests the user is drinking more than needed.
- Lay wording for "you may have had too much, too fast": headache, nausea or vomiting, bloating, confusion or unusual drowsiness after drinking a large amount in a short time. Action: stop drinking, do not "flush it out", and get medical help urgently if there is confusion, severe headache, repeated vomiting or a seizure.
- Because the two symptom sets overlap, the app should not interpret symptoms or tell the user which state they are in; it should present both lists in a static help screen and avoid symptom-triggered prompts, which would look like diagnosis (section 6).
- Urine colour guidance should be phrased as a rough self-check with the caveat about vitamins (B-complex) and medicines.

### Gaps
- No NHS, Mayo Clinic or Cleveland Clinic page specifically on overhydration was retrieved; the lay overhydration list rests on one review plus lower-quality consumer pages. The claim that clear urine and frequent urination are signs of overhydration comes only from commercial sites.
- No validated urine-colour chart source (for example Armstrong's 8-point scale) was retrieved.
