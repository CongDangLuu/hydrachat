# Official daily water intake recommendations and how to calculate / adjust a personal target (as of Nov 2026)

Reader's note on verification: the EFSA opinion (Wiley, HTTP 403) and the NCBI Bookshelf copy of the IOM tables (captcha) could not be opened directly. EFSA figures below come from the opinion's abstract as returned by search; IOM figures come from the National Academies' own online reader. The Vietnamese NIN 2016 handbook itself was not found online; its water table is quoted from a secondary article by a Vietnamese nutrition academic. Each such case is flagged inline.

## 1. What do WHO, EFSA, IOM / National Academies, and Vietnam's NIN / MoH recommend? (total water vs drinks)

### Takeaway
All major bodies set an *Adequate Intake* for **total water** (food moisture + all beverages), not a drinking-water requirement: EFSA 2.0 L (women) / 2.5 L (men); IOM 2.7 L / 3.7 L, of which about 2.2 L / 3.0 L is beverages. Vietnam's NIN 2016 handbook is reported to use ml/kg (40 / 35 / 30 by age and activity) and 1 ml/kcal, but this could only be verified through a secondary source.

### Cited Findings

**EFSA (2010)**
- Adequate Intakes for adults: 2.0 L/day for females (P95 3.1 L) and 2.5 L/day for males (P95 4.0 L); the same values apply to the elderly; adolescents from 14 are treated as adults. — [EFSA Journal 2010;8(3):1459](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1459) (abstract text via search; full text returned 403)
- These are **total water**: "water from drinking water, beverages of all kind, and from food moisture", and apply at moderate environmental temperature and moderate physical activity (PAL 1.6). — [EFSA Journal 2010;8(3):1459](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1459)
- Pregnancy: same as non-pregnant plus about 300 mL/day. Lactation: about 700 mL/day above the AI of non-lactating women. — [EFSA Journal 2010;8(3):1459](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1459)
- Basis: a combination of observed population intakes, desirable urine osmolarity, and desirable water volume per unit of energy consumed (not a measured requirement). — [EFSA Journal 2010;8(3):1459](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1459)

**US National Academies / IOM (2005)**
- AI for total water, ages 19-30: 3.7 L/day men, 2.7 L/day women. Fluids (drinking water + beverages) supply about 3.0 L (men) and 2.2 L (women), about 81% of total; food about 19%. — [National Academies, DRI for Water, ch. 4](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10925/chapter/6)
- A secondary review lists the same 3.7 / 2.7 L for all adults over 19 including over-70s (it mislabels them as WHO figures "supported by" the US National Academy of Medicine). — [Masot et al. 2020, Nutrients (PMC7694182)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7694182/)
- Pregnancy: 3.0 L/day total water, about 2.3 L (≈10 cups) as beverages. Lactation: 3.8 L/day total, about 3.1 L (≈13 cups) as beverages. — [National Academies, DRI for Water, ch. 4](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10925/chapter/6)
- The AI is the **median total water intake** in US survey data (NHANES III); hydration is maintained over a wide range of intakes, so the AI "should not be interpreted as a specific requirement". — [National Academies, DRI for Water, ch. 4](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10925/chapter/6)
- Thirst and normal drinking behaviour (especially with meals) generally maintain hydration over about 24 hours, though people under-replace losses in the short term. — [National Academies, DRI for Water, ch. 4](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10925/chapter/6)

**WHO**
- WHO has no formal nutrient reference value for water. The WHO-commissioned background paper (Grandjean, "Water Requirements, Impinging Factors, and Recommended Intakes") tabulates WHO working figures: adult female 2.2 L/day and adult male 2.9 L/day (sedentary, temperate); 4.5 L/day for both when physically active and/or at increased temperature (manual labour in high temperatures). The "optimal for disease prevention" column is left as "?". — [Grandjean, WHO background paper (readkong copy)](https://www.readkong.com/page/water-requirements-impinging-factors-and-recommended-6633990)
- Howard & Bartram (WHO 2003, *Domestic Water Quantity, Service Level and Health*): a minimum of 7.5 L per person per day covers consumption (hydration + food preparation) and basic hygiene for most people in most settings, derived from lactating women doing moderate activity in hot conditions. This is a water-supply planning figure, not a drinking target. — [pS-Eau abstract](https://www.pseau.org/outils/biblio/resume.php?d=2912)
- A later summary by the same authors puts hydration needs in hot, humid, labour-intensive settings at up to about 5.3 L/day for a lactating woman, against about 2-3 L/day in temperate conditions. — [PreventionWeb summary](https://www.preventionweb.net//node/16680)
- Vietnamese consumer pages attribute "2,700 ml women / 3,700 ml men from age 19" to WHO; those are the IOM figures. — [Vinmec](https://www.vinmec.com/vie/bai-viet/khuyen-cao-cua-who-ve-nhu-cau-nuoc-hang-ngay-vi); compare [National Academies](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10925/chapter/6)

**Vietnam (NIN / MoH)**
- The NIN handbook *Nhu cầu dinh dưỡng khuyến nghị cho người Việt Nam - 2016* is reported to give, by age and activity: adolescents 10-18: 40 ml/kg; "19-30 tuổi, hoạt động thể lực nặng": 40 ml/kg; "19-55 tuổi, hoạt động thể lực trung bình": 35 ml/kg; adults ≥55: 30 ml/kg. — [PGS.TS Phạm Văn Hoan, Viện Y học ứng dụng Việt Nam, 2017](https://vienyhocungdung.vn/vai-tro-cua-nuoc-uong-voi-co-the-va-nhu-cau-khuyen-nghi-hang-ngay-20170405092820074.htm)
- The same handbook is reported to give alternative methods: 1 ml per kcal for adults (1.5 ml/kcal for adolescents), 1500 ml/m² body surface, and for children 100 ml/kg (1-10 kg), 1000 ml + 50 ml/kg above 10 kg, 1500 ml + 20 ml/kg above 20 kg. — [Phạm Văn Hoan, VIAM, 2017](https://vienyhocungdung.vn/vai-tro-cua-nuoc-uong-voi-co-the-va-nhu-cau-khuyen-nghi-hang-ngay-20170405092820074.htm)
- The earlier legally binding table, Appendix 1 of MoH Circular 43/2014/TT-BYT ("Bảng nhu cầu dinh dưỡng khuyến nghị cho người Việt Nam"), contains **no water entry at all** (checked by extracting the document text: minerals and vitamins only). — [Circular 43/2014 appendix (thuvienphapluat)](https://cdn.thuvienphapluat.vn/phap-luat/2022-2/QD/250401/bang-nhu-cau-dinh-duong.docx)
- The HCMC Nutrition Research Institute's menu-planning guidance, citing the 2016 table, gives a simple 1.5-2 L/day of water for a normal adult. — [viendinhduongtphcm.org](https://viendinhduongtphcm.org/vi/dinh-duong-co-ban/xay-dung-khau-phan-cho-nguoi-binh-thuong.html) (search-result summary; page not opened)

### Inferences
- The widely repeated Vietnamese rule "40 ml/kg for 19-30, 35 ml/kg for 31-55, 30 ml/kg for >55" is a **simplification**. As reported, the 40 ml/kg adult figure is conditional on *heavy physical activity*; a sedentary office worker aged 19-55 falls in the 35 ml/kg band (which is itself labelled "moderate activity"), and ≥55 in the 30 ml/kg band.
- The app's 33 ml/kg sits between the Vietnamese 30 and 35 ml/kg bands, so it is consistent with the national handbook as reported, for the 19-55 group.
- International AIs differ by about 1 L (EFSA 2.0/2.5 vs IOM 2.7/3.7) because they are built from observed intakes in different populations, not from a measured physiological requirement. No figure is "the" correct one.

### Gaps
- The NIN 2016 handbook was not available online; whether its ml/kg values mean **total water or drinking water** is not stated in the secondary source (the article's context, which splits needs into 20-30% food and 70-80% drinks, suggests total water). Needs checking in the printed handbook.
- No pregnancy / lactation water values from a Vietnamese source were found.
- EFSA full text not opened; figures rely on the abstract.
- No Vietnamese national survey of actual water intake was retrieved (a 2024 Vietnamese journal article reporting low rates of adequate intake surfaced in search but was not read).

## 2. Share of total water from food and metabolic water, and what it implies for a "plain water" target

### Takeaway
Food typically supplies about 20-30% of total water intake in Western survey data, so a drinks target is roughly 70-80% of the total-water AI: about 1.6 L (women) / 2.0 L (men) from EFSA, 2.2 L / 3.0 L from IOM. Isotope data suggest the plain-water share can be much lower still.

### Cited Findings
- IOM: beverages about 81% of total water, food about 19% (US adults 19-30). — [National Academies, DRI for Water, ch. 4](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10925/chapter/6)
- US NFCS 1977-78: plain drinking water 31.4% of total water intake, other beverages 43.6%, food 25%. — [Grandjean, WHO background paper](https://www.readkong.com/page/water-requirements-impinging-factors-and-recommended-6633990)
- Vietnamese source: food contributes 20-30% and beverages 70-80% of daily water needs. — [Phạm Văn Hoan, VIAM, 2017](https://vienyhocungdung.vn/vai-tro-cua-nuoc-uong-voi-co-the-va-nhu-cau-khuyen-nghi-hang-ngay-20170405092820074.htm)
- Applying a 20% food share to EFSA's AIs gives 1.6 L/day (women) and 2.0 L/day (men) of drinks, which is exactly the ESPEN geriatric recommendation. — [Masot et al. 2020 (PMC7694182)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7694182/)
- Yamada et al. 2022 (Science, doubly-labelled-water data, 5,604 people): about 15% of water turnover is metabolic water plus water exchanged across body surfaces; a man in his 20s with a turnover of about 4.2 L therefore needs about 3.6 L of intake, and with a typical US/European diet over half of that comes from food, leaving about 1.5-1.8 L/day to drink; for a woman in her 20s about 1.3-1.4 L/day. — [University of Aberdeen release on Yamada et al.](https://www.abdn.ac.uk/about/international/news/16566/)
- The same authors say the "8 glasses / 2 L" rule is "probably too high for most people in most situations" and that it "confused total water turnover with water from beverages". — [University of Aberdeen](https://www.abdn.ac.uk/about/international/news/16566/); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/11/221125132035.htm)

### Inferences
- The Aberdeen "over half from food" statement conflicts with the 19-25% survey figures; the likely reason is that it counts all non-plain-water sources (food plus other beverages) versus plain water. The NFCS split (31% plain water, 44% other beverages, 25% food) supports this reading. For an app, this means the target must be defined: **"all drinks"** (about 75-80% of total water) or **"plain water only"** (could be as low as 30-50%).
- If the app counts only plain water, a target of 33 ml/kg will over-ask for users who also drink tea, coffee, soup broth, milk, etc. Letting users log other beverages toward the target is better aligned with every guideline than a plain-water-only goal.
- Vietnamese meals are comparatively water-rich (rice, soups/canh, noodle broths, fruit), so the food share is plausibly at the upper end of 20-30% or above. No measured Vietnamese figure was found; treat as an assumption.

### Gaps
- No Vietnam- or Southeast-Asia-specific measurement of water from food.
- Yamada's drinking-water estimates come from a press release paraphrase, not the paper's text.

## 3. Is a 30-35 ml/kg/day formula supported? Origin and weaknesses

### Takeaway
ml/kg formulas come from clinical nutrition / dietetics (fluid prescriptions for patients), not from the population reference values of EFSA, IOM or WHO; they are expert-opinion rules that have never been properly validated and they scale wrongly at the extremes of body weight. Vietnam's handbook does use ml/kg (see section 1).

### Cited Findings
- The 30 mL/kg/day rule is attributed to Chidester & Spangler (long-term-care dietetics); it is "not useful for thin or obese people" - it yields too little for thin people and too much for people with obesity. — [Masot et al. 2020 (PMC7694182)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7694182/)
- A tiered alternative (Skipper): 100 mL/kg for the first 10 kg, 50 mL/kg for the next 10 kg, 15 mL/kg for the remainder; judged the most suitable for older adults in that review, though still problematic in overweight people. — [Masot et al. 2020 (PMC7694182)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7694182/)
- Several incompatible bands circulate (25-30, 30-35, 30-40 mL/kg); at 70 kg they span 1,750-2,800 mL/day. — [DieteticCalc (low-authority aggregator)](https://dieteticcalc.com/equations/fluid-needs)
- Queensland Health's dietetic guidance notes many requirement equations are "based on 'expert opinion' or have limited supporting evidence" and that using adjusted body weight in overweight/obese patients is "highly debated". — [Queensland Health NEMO, Estimating requirements](https://www.health.qld.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0022/144175/est-req.pdf) (search-result summary; PDF not opened)
- UK NICE prescribes 25-30 mL/kg/day for hospital *maintenance* fluid; a 2024 Clinical Nutrition ESPEN paper argues this is too little water, and its authors disclose ties to fluid manufacturers. — [Hahn & Van Regenmortel 2024](https://www.clinicalnutritionespen.com/article/S2405-4577(24)00167-0/fulltext) (search-result summary)
- The US Food and Nutrition Board's older rule was 1 mL per kcal of energy expenditure (1945), "often increased to 1.5 mL/kcal" (1989) for activity, sweating and solute load. — [Grandjean, WHO background paper](https://www.readkong.com/page/water-requirements-impinging-factors-and-recommended-6633990)
- Grandjean cites a minimum fluid replacement for a 70 kg person in a temperate zone of 3 L/day (42.9 mL/kg) and 4.1-6 L/day in the tropics. — [Grandjean, WHO background paper](https://www.readkong.com/page/water-requirements-impinging-factors-and-recommended-6633990); the Vietnamese article quotes the same tropical 4.1-6 L but gives the temperate minimum as "khoảng 2 lít/ngày" — [Phạm Văn Hoan, VIAM](https://vienyhocungdung.vn/vai-tro-cua-nuoc-uong-voi-co-the-va-nhu-cau-khuyen-nghi-hang-ngay-20170405092820074.htm) (sources conflict)
- Armstrong & Johnson 2018: water requirements vary with body mass, dietary sodium, protein and total solute load, and day to day with activity and climate; there is no single daily requirement for a given person, and the AIs are median intakes, not requirements. — [Armstrong & Johnson 2018, Nutrients](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/10/12/1928)
- In Yamada's turnover model body weight carries only about 14 mL per kg, far below 30-35 mL/kg, with most of the turnover attached to a fixed base, activity level, sex, age and climate. — [Gigazine summary of the equation](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20221125-human-water-turnover) (coefficients not verified against the paper)

### Inferences
- A purely proportional formula (weight × constant) is physiologically too steep: body fat is low in water and does not raise needs in proportion. A 110 kg user gets 3,630 mL at 33 ml/kg, above the IOM beverage figure for men (3.0 L); a 42 kg user gets 1,400 mL, below the ESPEN/EFSA-derived 1.6 L floor for women. Hence clamping (section 5) matters more than the exact constant.
- Defensible alternatives for people with obesity: cap the weight used (e.g. at the weight corresponding to BMI 25-30 for the user's height), or use the tiered 100/50/15 rule (a 150 kg adult gets 3,450 mL versus 4,500-5,250 mL from a linear band, per [DieteticCalc](https://dieteticcalc.com/equations/fluid-needs)). Neither is validated; both are just less wrong than linear scaling.
- 33 ml/kg for typical Vietnamese office-worker weights (50-70 kg) yields 1,650-2,300 mL, which lands inside the range of drink-only figures from EFSA (1.6-2.0 L) and IOM (2.2-3.0 L). The current formula is reasonable in the middle of the weight range **if "target" means all drinks**.

### Gaps
- No ESPEN guideline for healthy adults endorsing 30-35 ml/kg was located; the ESPEN geriatric guideline uses fixed volumes (1.6 / 2.0 L), not ml/kg. The primary ESPEN text was not opened.
- No validation study of ml/kg rules against measured hydration status in healthy adults was found. A claim that "no fluid equation has been validated" (attributed to a 2021 paper) surfaced only through an aggregator and is unverified.
- The origin of the 30-35 ml/kg band (as opposed to 30 ml/kg) could not be traced to a primary source.

## 4. Adjustments: sex, age, body size, climate, air conditioning, activity, pregnancy, breastfeeding

### Takeaway
The largest drivers are physical activity and heat (litres per day), then sex (about 0.5 L), then pregnancy (+0.3 L) and lactation (+0.7 to +1.1 L); age lowers needs slightly but raises the *risk* of under-drinking. Evidence for an air-conditioning adjustment is essentially absent.

### Cited Findings
- **Drivers overall**: in 5,604 people, physical activity level and athletic status explained the most variance in water turnover, followed by sex, Human Development Index and age; measured turnover ranged about 1-6 L/day. — [ScienceDaily on Yamada et al. 2022](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/11/221125132035.htm)
- **Sex**: all else equal, men and women differ by about 0.5 L/day of turnover. — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/11/221125132035.htm). AIs differ by 0.5 L (EFSA) or 1.0 L (IOM). — [EFSA](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1459); [National Academies](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10925/chapter/6)
- **Age**: turnover peaks in men 20-35 (about 4.2 L/day) and women 20-40 (about 3.3 L/day), falling to about 2.5 L/day by age 90. — [University of Aberdeen](https://www.abdn.ac.uk/about/international/news/16566/)
- **Older adults**: weaker and later thirst, reduced urine-concentrating ability, lower ADH; ESPEN recommends at least 1.6 L/day of drinks for older women and 2.0 L/day for older men unless a clinical condition requires otherwise. EFSA keeps the adult AI for the elderly. — [Masot et al. 2020 (PMC7694182)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7694182/); [EFSA](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1459). Note one secondary source describes the ESPEN 1.6/2.0 L as including food; the drinks-only reading is the more common one (source conflict; primary text not opened).
- **Heart failure exception**: cardiology societies limit fluid to 1.5-2.0 L/day in moderate/severe heart failure. — [Masot et al. 2020 (PMC7694182)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7694182/)
- **Physical activity**: resting men need about 2.5 L/day total water, about 3.2 L/day with modest activity; men doing more than 60 min/day of activity need about 3.0-4.5 L/day. — [National Academies, ch. 4](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10925/chapter/6)
- **Heat**: tropical or desert conditions can add 2-7 L/day of sweat loss; active people continually exposed to hot weather often need 6-8 L/day or more; at about 40 °C temperate-climate requirements can double or triple. — [National Academies, ch. 4](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10925/chapter/6)
- **WHO working figures**: 2.2 L (women) / 2.9 L (men) sedentary-temperate versus 4.5 L for manual labour in high temperature. Sweat rates can reach 3-4 L/hour in extreme exertion. — [Grandjean, WHO background paper](https://www.readkong.com/page/water-requirements-impinging-factors-and-recommended-6633990)
- **Humidity / development**: in Yamada's model a 50-point rise in relative humidity adds about 0.3 L/day; people in low-HDI countries have higher turnover, which the authors speculate reflects less air conditioning and heating (i.e. climate control *reduces* water turnover). — [Gigazine](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20221125-human-water-turnover); [University of Aberdeen](https://www.abdn.ac.uk/about/international/news/16566/); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/11/221125132035.htm)
- **Air-conditioned environment**: the one controlled study found (49 healthy men in Singapore, 8 h in a room at about 24.4 °C / 55% RH) compared beverages rather than testing whether AC raises needs; its authors caution against extrapolation. — [Siow et al. 2017, Nutrients (PMC5372905)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5372905/)
- **Pregnancy**: +300 mL/day (EFSA); IOM AI 3.0 L total, about 2.3 L as beverages. — [EFSA](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1459); [National Academies](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10925/chapter/6)
- **Breastfeeding**: +700 mL/day (EFSA); IOM AI 3.8 L total, about 3.1 L as beverages (about +0.9 L of beverages versus non-lactating women). — [EFSA](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1459); [National Academies](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10925/chapter/6)
- **Vietnamese handbook (as reported)**: activity and age are folded into the ml/kg constant - 40 (heavy activity, 19-30), 35 (moderate, 19-55), 30 (≥55). — [Phạm Văn Hoan, VIAM](https://vienyhocungdung.vn/vai-tro-cua-nuoc-uong-voi-co-the-va-nhu-cau-khuyen-nghi-hang-ngay-20170405092820074.htm)

### Inferences
- Suggested adjustment set for the app, each traceable to a source above: women's baseline about 0.5 L below men's at equal weight (or simply keep the weight formula, which already yields lower targets for lighter users); age ≥55-60: constant 30 instead of 33-35 ml/kg, but never below the 1.6 / 2.0 L floor; pregnancy +300 mL; breastfeeding +700 mL; exercise: add per session rather than raising the daily constant (the commonly used "replace sweat loss" logic; a per-hour figure was not sourced here); hot outdoor exposure: the largest and least predictable adjustment, better offered as an opt-in "outdoor / hot day" bump than applied to everyone in Vietnam.
- For an office worker who commutes in the heat but spends 8+ hours in air conditioning, the tropical "4.1-6 L/day" figures do not apply; those describe continuous heat exposure and physical work. The temperate / sedentary figures (EFSA, IOM, WHO 2.2-2.9 L total) are the appropriate baseline, perhaps slightly raised.
- "Air conditioning dehydrates you, so drink more" is a common app talking point with no retrieved evidence behind it; the population data point the other way (climate control lowers turnover). The honest framing is that AC blunts thirst cues and people sit for hours without drinking, which is a *reminder* problem rather than a *higher requirement*.
- Illustrative only: plugging a sedentary 30-year-old (PAL 1.5, sea level, 27 °C mean, 80% RH, high-HDI) into the Yamada equation as reported by Gigazine gives a turnover of roughly 3.4 L/day for a 55 kg woman and 3.9 L/day for a 65 kg man. Turnover is not drinking volume (subtract about 15% metabolic/surface water, then food and other beverages), the model uses outdoor climate, and the coefficients were not verified against the paper, so this should not be used to set targets.

### Gaps
- No quantitative guideline adjustment for air conditioning or low indoor humidity was found.
- No per-hour exercise fluid figure was sourced (ACSM position stands were not searched).
- Yamada equation coefficients and the HDI coding come from a tech-news summary, not the paper or supplement.
- No Vietnam-specific sweat-loss or water-balance study was found.

## 5. Defensible minimum and maximum caps for a daily drinking-water target

### Takeaway
No body sets an upper limit for water, and none sets a hard minimum; a defensible consumer range is roughly 1.5-1.6 L as a floor and 3.0-3.5 L as a ceiling for the baseline target, with any heat or exercise bonus added on top and an hourly pacing limit well under 0.7-1.0 L/hour.

### Cited Findings
- IOM set **no Tolerable Upper Intake Level** for water, because healthy people excrete the excess; acute toxicity arises when intake outpaces renal excretion, roughly 0.7-1.0 L/hour. Hyponatremia from excess hypotonic fluid with low sodium intake occurs only "in very unusual circumstances". — [National Academies, ch. 4](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10925/chapter/6)
- Maximum renal excretion in older people is also quoted as about 0.7-1.0 L/h, and older kidneys handle water loads less well. — [Masot et al. 2020 (PMC7694182)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7694182/)
- Floor candidates: ESPEN at least 1.6 L (women) / 2.0 L (men) of drinks for older adults; EFSA AI minus 20% food = 1.6 / 2.0 L. — [Masot et al. 2020 (PMC7694182)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7694182/)
- Lower estimates of what actually needs to be drunk: about 1.3-1.4 L (young women) and 1.5-1.8 L (young men), from isotope turnover data. — [University of Aberdeen](https://www.abdn.ac.uk/about/international/news/16566/)
- Ceiling candidates: IOM beverage component 3.0 L (men), 3.1 L (lactating women); EFSA 95th percentile of *total* water intake 3.1 L (women) / 4.0 L (men). — [National Academies](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10925/chapter/6); [EFSA](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1459)
- Popular-health sources say symptoms of water intoxication tend to appear after more than 3-4 L within a few hours, and advise no more than about 1 L/hour. — [Healthline](https://www.healthline.com/health/how-much-water-can-kill-you) (low-authority; search summary)
- In one small trial, raising water intake in healthy adults *increased* daytime blood pressure slightly, and reducing fluid intake improved urinary frequency, urgency and nocturia, so "more" is not costless for everyone. — [Hakam et al. 2024, JAMA Netw Open (PMC11589796)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11589796/)

### Inferences
- Proposed clamp for the baseline (all-drinks) target: **minimum 1,500-1,600 mL, maximum 3,000-3,500 mL**. Floor rationale: ESPEN/EFSA-derived 1.6 L for women, isotope-based 1.3-1.8 L. Ceiling rationale: IOM beverage figure for men (3.0 L) and for lactation (3.1 L); 3.5 L leaves headroom for large, active men. Heat/exercise add-ons can exceed the ceiling but should be explicit and time-bound.
- With 33 ml/kg the clamp bites below about 46-48 kg (floor) and above about 91-106 kg (ceiling), which is exactly where the linear formula is least trustworthy.
- Pacing guard: never suggest "catch-up" drinking of more than about 500 mL in an hour; this stays below the 0.7-1.0 L/h excretion ceiling with a safety margin and avoids evening catch-up that causes nocturia. The 500 mL figure is a design judgement, not a sourced threshold.
- The app should carry a short disclaimer that people with heart failure, kidney disease, liver disease, or on fluid restriction / diuretics must follow their clinician's limit, since a reminder app pushing 2+ L is directly contrary to a 1.5-2.0 L restriction.

### Gaps
- No guideline states a numeric maximum daily drinking volume for healthy adults; daily renal maxima quoted online (15-28 L) come from popular sites and were not verified.
- No primary clinical review of hyponatremia thresholds (e.g. StatPearls, exercise-associated hyponatremia consensus) was opened.

## 6. Strength of evidence that drinking more improves health in already-healthy adults

### Takeaway
Evidence is good only for people with a specific problem: recurrent kidney stones and recurrent UTI in women who drink little. For healthy adults who already drink to thirst, benefits for weight, headache, kidney function or general wellbeing rest on a few small trials and are weak or unproven.

### Cited Findings
- Systematic review of RCTs (Hakam et al. 2024, JAMA Network Open): only 18 RCTs (n = 14-631) from 1,464 records; "the quality and quantity of evidence was limited"; 50% low risk of bias, 27% some concerns, 22% high risk; many outcomes examined by a single study. Conclusion: a small number of studies suggest benefit for weight loss and nephrolithiasis; single studies raise the possibility of benefit for migraine, UTI, diabetes and hypotension. — [Hakam et al. 2024 (PMC11589796)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11589796/)
- **Kidney stones**: Borghi 1996, 5-year RCT in first-time calcium stone formers; water intake to reach urine volume ≥2 L/day; recurrence 12/99 vs 27/100 (p = 0.008); time to recurrence 38.7 vs 25.1 months. This is secondary prevention in stone formers, not healthy adults. AUA/EAU now recommend at least 2.5 L/day fluid for stone formers. — [Seminal papers in urology: Borghi 1996 (PMC10838424)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10838424/)
- **UTI**: Hooton 2018, 12-month open-label RCT, 140 premenopausal women with recurrent cystitis drinking under 1.5 L/day; adding 1.5 L/day cut episodes from 3.2 to 1.7 (difference 1.5, 95% CI 1.2-1.8) and antibiotic courses from 3.6 to 1.9. Limits: unblinded, self-reported, low-drinkers only; optimal volume and benefit in lower-risk women unknown. A second small trial (n = 14) found no difference. — [MDedge summary of Hooton 2018](https://mdedge.com/content/increased-daily-water-intake-benefits-premenopausal-women-recurrent-utis); [Hakam et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11589796/)
- **Kidney function**: CKD WIT (Clark 2018, JAMA), stage 3 CKD, coaching to drink more for 1 year: eGFR change -2.2 vs -1.9 mL/min/1.73 m², adjusted difference -0.3 (95% CI -1.8 to 1.2; p = 0.74) - no benefit; urine volume rose only about 0.6 L/day and the trial may have been underpowered. — [Karger review of CKD WIT](https://karger.com/anm/article/76/Suppl.%201/69/52174/Early-Coaching-to-Increase-Water-Intake-in-CKD); [MDedge](https://hospitalist.qa00.mdedge.com/rheumatology/article/165091/nephrology/even-year-increased-water-intake-did-not-change-ckd-course)
- **Weight**: three trials of about 500 mL water *before meals* in adults with overweight/obesity showed more weight loss (Parretti 2015: 1.3 kg more, 95% CI 0.14-2.4; Akers 2012 and Dennis 2010: 44-87% greater loss); an adolescent trial showed no effect. Effect is tied to pre-meal timing, not to total daily volume. — [Hakam et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11589796/); [Healio](https://cm.healio.com/news/primary-care/20241216/simple-and-ubiquitous-increasing-water-intake-may-help-several-health-conditions)
- **Headache**: Spigt 2012 (n = 102, recurrent headache) improved a migraine quality-of-life score by 4.5 points (p = 0.007) but **not** the number of headache days (p = 0.82); Spigt 2005 (n = 18) found no significant effect. Subjective outcome, unblinded. — [Hakam et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11589796/)
- **Glucose**: one trial (n = 40, type 2 diabetes) showed lower fasting glucose; another (n = 60) showed no change. — [Hakam et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11589796/)
- **Reference bodies**: IOM set its AI from median intake precisely because no intake level could be tied to a health outcome; the WHO background paper leaves "optimal intake for disease prevention" as "?". — [National Academies](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10925/chapter/6); [Grandjean](https://www.readkong.com/page/water-requirements-impinging-factors-and-recommended-6633990)

### Inferences
- The app should not promise disease prevention, weight loss, better skin, detox or sharper thinking. Claims that are defensible: staying adequately hydrated, replacing losses in heat and exercise, and (worded carefully) that people prone to kidney stones or recurrent UTIs are medically advised to drink more.
- Because benefit is concentrated in **low drinkers**, the most valuable product behaviour is lifting people who drink very little up to an adequate level, not pushing already-adequate drinkers toward ever higher targets. This argues for a moderate target with a floor, and against streak mechanics that reward exceeding the target.
- Urine colour / frequency and thirst are more individual signals than any formula; presenting the number as a "starting estimate you can adjust" matches the evidence better than presenting it as a requirement.

### Gaps
- **Cognition and mood** were not researched in this pass; no sources retrieved. (Short-term dehydration studies exist, but whether extra water helps already-hydrated adults was not checked.)
- No evidence was retrieved on primary prevention of stones or UTI in healthy, never-affected adults.
- Hooton 2018 and CKD WIT were read through secondary summaries, not the JAMA papers.
- Observational associations (e.g. serum sodium / hydration and ageing or chronic disease) were not reviewed.
